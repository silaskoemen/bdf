\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}

This section evaluates Bayesian Distributional Forests (BDF) against a suite of probabilistic tabular baselines on four axes: (i) real-data benchmarks covering regression and classification (Section~\ref{subsec:exp_real}), (ii) controlled synthetic data-generating processes that probe distributional shape recovery (Section~\ref{subsec:exp_synthetic}), (iii) the choice of split-scoring criterion (Section~\ref{subsec:exp_scoring}), and (iv) computational complexity (Section~\ref{subsec:exp_complexity}). Additional ablations—parameter sensitivity, misspecification, noise features, convergence behavior, ensemble size, and conformalization comparisons—are deferred to Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results}.

% ==========================================
% 5.1 REAL-DATA BENCHMARKS
% ==========================================
\subsection{Real-data benchmarks}\label{subsec:exp_real}

\paragraph{Protocol.}
We evaluate on a collection of tabular regression and classification datasets sourced from UCI and OpenML; Appendix~\ref{app:experimental-setup} documents the full list, preprocessing, hyperparameter grids, and tuning protocol. All methods are tuned with the same compute budget, and every result is aggregated over multiple folds with standard errors reported in the appendix.

\subsubsection{Regression}\label{subsubsec:exp_regression}

Figure~\ref{fig:crps-rel-to-best} summarizes probabilistic accuracy via CRPS relative to the per-dataset best model. BDF attains the lowest average relative CRPS, outperforming the second-best (conformalized LightGBM) by roughly $7.7\%$ and NGBoost by $14.6\%$. The critical-difference diagram in Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results} (Figure~\ref{fig:cd-crps}) shows that CatBoost-with-Uncertainty attains a marginally lower average rank, but its relative-to-best gap is larger ($1.28$ vs.\ $1.16$), indicating that BDF is more consistently close to the per-dataset optimum even when it does not outright win.

% HEADLINE PLOT: Coverage vs. interval-score rank.
\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{plots/regression/regression_coverage_vs_iscore_rank.pdf}
    \caption{Empirical coverage at the 50/90/95\% levels versus average interval-score rank. BDF achieves nominal-to-slightly-conservative coverage at the lowest interval-score rank.}
    \label{fig:coverage-vs-interval-score-rank}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:coverage-vs-interval-score-rank} positions each method by its empirical coverage against the average interval-score rank. BDF simultaneously attains the best interval-score rank and coverage close to the nominal levels, slightly on the conservative side. Point-accurate competitors such as CatBoost-with-Uncertainty and NGBoost fall substantially below nominal coverage, reflecting the standard sharpness--calibration tradeoff. Conformalized forests achieve nominal coverage but at the cost of CRPS and interval score. PIT histograms in Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results} (Figure~\ref{fig:pit-histograms}) corroborate this picture: BDF is near-uniform with mild central mass; CatBoost and NGBoost accumulate mass at $\{0,1\}$.

\paragraph{Point accuracy.}
BDF is not optimized for point prediction; on RMSE it sits roughly $22\%$ off the per-dataset best, behind CatBoost and LightGBM. We view this as the expected cost of a distributional objective rather than a failure mode; the full RMSE comparison and scatterplots are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results} (Figures~\ref{fig:rmse-rel-to-best}, \ref{fig:regression-scatter}).

\subsection{Synthetic data-generating processes}\label{subsec:exp_synthetic}

We construct five synthetic DGPs---\emph{heteroscedastic sinusoidal}, \emph{bimodal mixture}, \emph{heavy-tailed}, \emph{step function}, and \emph{sparse sampling}---chosen to isolate shape phenomena (heteroscedasticity, multimodality, heavy tails, discontinuities, local data sparsity) that stress different components of a probabilistic predictor. Full DGP definitions are given in Appendix~\ref{app:experimental-setup}.

% HEADLINE PLOT: predictions grid across all DGPs and models.
\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{plots/synthetic/summary/predictions_grid.pdf}
    \caption{Predicted conditional distributions across DGPs (rows) and methods (columns) on a representative fold. Shaded bands show 90\% central predictive intervals; solid lines show predictive means.}
    \label{fig:synthetic-predictions-grid}
\end{figure}

% HEADLINE PLOT: calibration curves aggregated across DGPs.
\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{plots/synthetic/summary/calibration_curves.pdf}
    \caption{Average calibration across synthetic DGPs. BDF tracks the diagonal closely; CatBoost-UQ and NGBoost are systematically overconfident.}
    \label{fig:synthetic-calibration}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:synthetic-predictions-grid} compares predicted conditional distributions across DGPs and methods. Several patterns emerge. On the \emph{bimodal mixture} DGP, only BDF recovers a visibly bimodal predictive density: the forest mixture~\eqref{eq:forest_mixture} over randomized partitions assigns mass to both modes whenever different trees isolate different component regions, whereas unimodal baselines (NGBoost, CatBoost-UQ) collapse to the mean of the mixture and underestimate tail mass. On the \emph{step function} DGP, BDF produces sharp transitions aligned with the discontinuities---a direct consequence of evidence-based splitting, which accepts partitions whenever the log Bayes factor exceeds the depth/multiplicity threshold (Section~\ref{subsec:theory_split_bf})---while smooth methods (Gaussian process baselines, Bayesian ridge) spread uncertainty across the discontinuity.

On the \emph{heavy-tailed} DGP, BDF with Student-$t$ leaves attains near-nominal coverage with tighter interval width than Gaussian-leaf variants, confirming that leaf-family choice controls tail behavior without requiring global distributional assumptions. The \emph{heteroscedastic sinusoidal} and \emph{sparse sampling} DGPs stress local variance estimation: the per-leaf posterior on the scale parameter inflates predictive width where data are sparse, as desired, while contracting it where they are dense. The aggregated calibration curve (Figure~\ref{fig:synthetic-calibration}) summarizes these patterns: BDF tracks the diagonal closely at all nominal levels, whereas CatBoost-UQ and NGBoost are systematically overconfident. Per-DGP spread--skill plots, conditional calibration heatmaps, and PIT histograms are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:synthetic-diagnostics}.

\subsection{Split-scoring criterion: NLE vs.\ BIC}\label{subsec:exp_scoring}

A core design choice in BDF is the use of the negative log evidence (NLE) for conjugate leaves and its BIC surrogate otherwise (Section~\ref{subsec:split_scoring}). We verify empirically that (i) NLE and BIC agree asymptotically on conjugate families, and (ii) NLE provides a non-trivial advantage in the small-leaf regime where BIC's $O(1)$ remainder is non-negligible.

% HEADLINE PLOT 1: interaction plot showing NLE vs. BIC across sample sizes.
\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{plots/scoring_method/regression/fig1_regression_interaction.pdf}
    \caption{Regression: interaction between split criterion (NLE vs.\ BIC) and sample size. NLE dominates in small-leaf regimes; the gap closes as $n_\ell$ grows, consistent with Section~\ref{subsec:theory_surrogates}.}
    \label{fig:scoring-regression-interaction}
\end{figure}

% HEADLINE PLOT 2: CD diagram summarizing scoring comparison on core datasets.
\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{plots/scoring_method/regression/fig3_cd_diagram_core.pdf}
    \caption{Critical-difference diagram of scoring-method variants on core regression datasets.}
    \label{fig:scoring-regression-cd}
\end{figure}

% HEADLINE PLOT 3: classification counterpart.
\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{plots/scoring_method/classification/fig1_classification_interaction.pdf}
    \caption{Classification counterpart of Figure~\ref{fig:scoring-regression-interaction}.}
    \label{fig:scoring-classification-interaction}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:scoring-regression-interaction} confirms the theoretical prediction of Section~\ref{subsec:theory_surrogates}: NLE and BIC converge in ranking and acceptance behavior as leaf sample size grows, and their gap is concentrated in the small-$n_\ell$ regime where the $O(1)$ remainder in~\eqref{eq:laplace_bic_theory} is numerically comparable to the fit improvement. In this regime NLE dominates because it accounts for posterior shrinkage toward the prior via the Occam factor in~\eqref{eq:evidence_decomposition}, whereas BIC applies a fixed $\tfrac{k}{2}\log n_\ell$ penalty that underweights this effect. The critical-difference diagram in Figure~\ref{fig:scoring-regression-cd} shows NLE outperforming BIC on core regression datasets at the 5\% significance level; the classification counterpart (Figure~\ref{fig:scoring-classification-interaction}) exhibits the same qualitative pattern but with a smaller absolute gap, reflecting the less pronounced small-leaf Occam effect for the Beta--Bernoulli family.

The practical implication is straightforward: conjugate families should use NLE whenever closed-form evidence is available, while non-conjugate leaves may rely on BIC with minimal loss at moderate $n_\ell$. Robustness-split CD diagrams (Figures~\ref{fig:scoring-regression-cd-robust},~\ref{fig:scoring-classification-cd-robust}) and real-data comparisons (Figure~\ref{fig:scoring-real-data-cd}) in Appendix~\ref{app:scoring-method-full} corroborate this recommendation across heterogeneous data regimes.

Figure~\ref{fig:complexity-scaling} reports wall-clock fit time as a function of dataset size. BDF scales comparably to LightGBM and is tens of times faster than NGBoost, BART, and CatBoost-UQ on the same hardware and hyperparameter budget. Three design choices explain this profile: (i) sufficient-statistic leaf updates are $O(1)$ per observation (Appendix~\ref{app:conjugate_updates}); (ii) the fixed-resolution candidate cutpoint grid bounds the split search at each node by $K \cdot \lfloor 1/\eta\rfloor$, independent of $n$ (Section~\ref{subsec:complexity_growth}); and (iii) trees are trained independently and admit trivial parallelization over the ensemble. Memory usage and the overhead ratio relative to a non-probabilistic GBDT baseline are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:complexity-additional} (Figure~\ref{fig:complexity-extras}).

Beyond the four axes covered in this section, we run six additional diagnostic experiments: parameter sensitivity (Appendix~\ref{app:additional-param-sensitivity}), likelihood misspecification (Appendix~\ref{app:misspecification}), robustness to noise features (Appendix~\ref{app:noise}), convergence and learning curves (Appendix~\ref{app:convergence}), ensemble size (Appendix~\ref{app:ensemble-size}), and post-hoc conformalization (Appendix~\ref{app:conformalization}). These experiments confirm the qualitative findings above and characterize the regions of parameter and data space where BDF's advantages are largest.
