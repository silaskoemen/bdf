\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}

This section evaluates BDF on real-data regression and classification benchmarks, controlled synthetic DGPs, split-scoring ablations, and computational complexity. Additional diagnostics are in Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results}.

% ==========================================
% 5.1 REAL-DATA BENCHMARKS
% ==========================================
\subsection{Real-data benchmarks}\label{subsec:exp_real}

\paragraph{Protocol.}
We evaluate on UCI and OpenML tabular datasets; Appendix~\ref{app:experimental-setup} documents preprocessing, model grids, tuning, and fold protocol. All methods use the same tuning budget and processed numeric matrices, incomplete variant/dataset pairs are excluded rather than imputed, and reported summaries aggregate folds 1--9 after fold-0 selection. The regression comparison includes QRF and DRF as strong nonparametric forest baselines and fused XGBoostLSS variants selected by fold-0 CRPS; the classification comparison focuses on probabilistic classifiers with predicted class probabilities.

\subsubsection{Regression}\label{subsubsec:exp_regression}

% HEADLINE PLOT: CRPS relative to best (average).
\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{plots/regression/regression_rel_to_best_crps.pdf}
    \caption{Average CRPS relative to the per-dataset best model. Lower is better; $1.0$ would indicate the best CRPS on every dataset.}
    \label{fig:crps-rel-to-best}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:crps-rel-to-best} and Table~\ref{tab:rel-to-best-crps} summarize CRPS relative to the per-dataset best model. QRF is the strongest aggregate CRPS/CRPSS method, with average relative CRPS $1.270$ and average CRPSS rank $2.13$ (Table~\ref{tab:rankings-crpss}); DRF is second by both summaries. BDF-Full is the strongest parametric probabilistic model by relative CRPS ($1.385$), ahead of conformalized LightGBM ($1.432$), CatBoost-with-Uncertainty ($1.444$), XGBoostLSS ($3.085$), and the remaining baselines. The sign test and critical-difference diagram (Table~\ref{tab:win-tie-loss}, Figure~\ref{fig:cd-crps}) show statistically supported CRPS advantages for QRF/DRF over BDF-Full, so BDF's value is not universal CRPS dominance but an explicit likelihood interface near the probabilistic-score frontier.

\paragraph{Which leaf family and split score are actually selected?}
Table~\ref{tab:bdf-selection} reports the BDF leaf family and split-score variant chosen by fold-0 CRPS. These counts are descriptive outcomes of the pre-specified tuning split rather than estimates of selection probabilities under repeated tuning splits. Regression selects Student-$t$ leaves on 9 of 15 datasets and Normal--Normal leaves on 6. Exact NLE is selected on 1 dataset, NLL+BIC on 7, and pure plug-in NLL on 7, supporting the framing of BDF as evidence-centered rather than strictly conjugate-Bayesian. The tuned Normal--Normal variant is close to the selected variant on most datasets (median CRPS gap $+0.81\%$; Table~\ref{tab:bdf-conjugate-vs-selected-gap}), whereas the fixed-NLE Normal--Normal Core reference in Table~\ref{tab:bdf-normal-nle-vs-full} gives the stricter exact-evidence comparison; its relative-CRPS column is normalized within that comparison set, unlike the full-benchmark relative CRPS in Table~\ref{tab:rel-to-best-crps}. Table~\ref{tab:bdf-student-t-stability} separates the two Student-$t$ score choices.

Figure~\ref{fig:coverage-vs-interval-score-rank} positions empirical coverage against average interval-score rank. BDF-Full has the best average 90\% interval-score rank with close-to-nominal, slightly conservative coverage; QRF is similarly calibrated and stronger on CRPS. Point-accurate competitors such as CatBoost-with-Uncertainty and NGBoost under-cover, while conformalized forests recover coverage at the cost of CRPS and interval score.

\paragraph{Point accuracy.}
BDF is not optimized for point prediction, but average relative RMSE is informative: CatBoost-with-Uncertainty and conformalized LightGBM lead, while BDF-Full follows at $1.349$ and remains ahead of QRF ($1.420$) and DRF ($1.513$) (Figure~\ref{fig:rmse-rel-to-best}). Thus BDF-Full sits between nonparametric distributional forests on CRPS and boosted-tree methods on point accuracy.

\subsection{Synthetic data-generating processes}\label{subsec:exp_synthetic}

We construct five synthetic DGPs---\emph{heteroscedastic sinusoidal}, \emph{bimodal mixture}, \emph{heavy-tailed}, \emph{step function}, and \emph{sparse sampling}---chosen to isolate shape phenomena (heteroscedasticity, multimodality, heavy tails, discontinuities, local data sparsity) that stress different components of a probabilistic predictor. Full DGP definitions are given in Appendix~\ref{app:experimental-setup}.

% HEADLINE PLOT: predictions grid across all DGPs and models.
\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{plots/synthetic/summary/predictions_grid.pdf}
    \caption{Predicted conditional distributions across DGPs (rows) and methods (columns) on a representative fold. Shaded bands show 90\% central predictive intervals; solid lines show predictive means.}
    \label{fig:synthetic-predictions-grid}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:synthetic-predictions-grid} compares predicted conditional distributions across DGPs and methods. The synthetic results mirror the real-data picture: BDF variants recover useful distributional structure and maintain near-nominal coverage, but QRF/DRF remain strong nonparametric baselines and can match or exceed BDF by CRPS. In particular, BDF-KDE captures the bimodal shape among explicit predictive-distribution models, while DRF has the lowest CRPS on that DGP; on the step-function DGP, BDF tracks discontinuities sharply, while QRF is marginally best by CRPS. Summary diagnostics are in Appendix~\ref{app:synthetic-diagnostics}.

% 5.3 SCORING METHOD ABLATION
% ==========================================
\subsection{Split-scoring criterion: NLE vs.\ BIC}\label{subsec:exp_scoring}

The scoring ablation asks whether NLE, NLL+BIC, and pure NLL materially change the fitted predictor. At matched hyperparameters, Table~\ref{tab:a2_real_data} shows close agreement among the retained scores on conjugate leaves. On the full regression benchmark, however, fold-0 tuning often selects evidence surrogates or plug-in scores (Table~\ref{tab:bdf-selection}), especially with Student-$t$ leaves (Table~\ref{tab:bdf-student-t-stability}). Table~\ref{tab:bdf-leaf-score-ablation} isolates this effect under fixed BDF configurations, with its relative-CRPS column normalized within the fixed-ablation set: the Normal--Normal scores are close in aggregate CRPS, with plug-in NLL and NLL+BIC slightly ahead of fixed NLE, while Student-$t$ leaves are competitive under both pure NLL and NLL+BIC, with pure NLL giving the best average interval-score rank. We therefore treat pure NLL selections as useful regularized heuristics inside the full BDF architecture, not as evidence of exact Bayesian Student-$t$ inference.

Figure~\ref{fig:complexity-scaling} reports wall-clock fit and prediction time as functions of sample size, feature count, and ensemble size for BDF and a random-forest reference implementation; Table~\ref{tab:complexity} reports the corresponding empirical scaling exponents and overhead ratios. Three design choices explain this profile: (i) sufficient-statistic leaf updates are $O(1)$ per observation for conjugate leaves (Appendix~\ref{app:conjugate_updates}); (ii) the fixed-resolution candidate cutpoint grid bounds the split search at each node by $K \cdot \lfloor 1/\eta\rfloor$, independent of $n$ (Section~\ref{subsec:complexity_growth}); and (iii) trees are trained independently and admit trivial parallelization over the ensemble. Memory usage and the overhead ratio relative to the same random-forest reference are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:complexity-additional} (Figure~\ref{fig:complexity-extras}).

\paragraph{Ablation overview.}
Beyond the four axes covered in this section, we run seven additional diagnostic experiments: conditional calibration by predicted uncertainty (Appendix~\ref{app:conditional-diagnostics}), parameter sensitivity (Appendix~\ref{app:additional-param-sensitivity}), likelihood misspecification (Appendix~\ref{app:misspecification}), robustness to noise features (Appendix~\ref{app:noise}), convergence and learning curves (Appendix~\ref{app:convergence}), ensemble size (Appendix~\ref{app:ensemble-size}), and post-hoc conformalization (Appendix~\ref{app:conformalization}). These experiments confirm the qualitative findings above and characterize the regions of parameter and data space where BDF's advantages are largest.
