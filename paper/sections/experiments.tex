\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}

This section evaluates Bayesian Distributional Forests (BDF) against a suite of probabilistic tabular baselines on four axes: (i) real-data benchmarks covering regression and classification (Section~\ref{subsec:exp_real}), (ii) controlled synthetic data-generating processes that probe distributional shape recovery (Section~\ref{subsec:exp_synthetic}), (iii) the choice of split-scoring criterion (Section~\ref{subsec:exp_scoring}), and (iv) computational complexity (Section~\ref{subsec:exp_complexity}). Additional ablations---conditional calibration by predicted uncertainty, parameter sensitivity, misspecification, noise features, convergence behavior, ensemble size, and conformalization comparisons---are deferred to Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results}.

% ==========================================
% 5.1 REAL-DATA BENCHMARKS
% ==========================================
\subsection{Real-data benchmarks}\label{subsec:exp_real}

\paragraph{Protocol.}
We evaluate on a collection of tabular regression and classification datasets sourced from UCI and OpenML; Appendix~\ref{app:experimental-setup} documents the full list, preprocessing, hyperparameter grids, and tuning protocol. All methods are tuned with the same compute budget, and every result is aggregated over multiple folds with standard errors reported in the appendix. The numerical settings called \texttt{tuning\_sample\_size}=100 and \texttt{sample\_size}=1000 refer to predictive draws or quantile grid evaluations used for CRPS and interval metrics, not to the number of training or test observations.
The main real-data tables emphasize probabilistic tree, boosting, Bayesian, conformal, QRF/DRF, and XGBoostLSS baselines with stable benchmark implementations; Appendix~\ref{app:experimental-setup-models} records the exact model set and implementation caveats.
This baseline set is intentionally conservative for the revised claim: QRF and DRF are included as strong nonparametric forest competitors for regression; XGBoostLSS is reported as a fused distributional-boosting baseline whose Gaussian, Student-$t$, Laplace, and Gaussian-mixture variants are selected by the same fold-0 CRPS protocol; and incomplete variant/dataset pairs are excluded rather than imputed. QRF and DRF are not added to the binary classification study because the submitted comparison there targets probabilistic classifiers with predicted class probabilities, whereas these baselines enter this paper as regression distributional predictors; RF, LightGBM, and their calibrated variants cover the corresponding tree-based classification comparison.

Figure~\ref{fig:crps-rel-to-best} and Table~\ref{tab:rel-to-best-crps} summarize probabilistic accuracy via CRPS relative to the per-dataset best model. QRF is the strongest aggregate CRPS/CRPSS method, with average relative CRPS $1.270$ and average CRPSS rank $2.13$ (Table~\ref{tab:rankings-crpss}); DRF is second by both summaries. BDF is the strongest parametric probabilistic model by relative CRPS ($1.391$), ahead of conformalized LightGBM ($1.432$), CatBoost-with-Uncertainty ($1.444$), XGBoostLSS ($3.084$), and the remaining baselines. The per-dataset sign test and critical-difference diagram (Table~\ref{tab:win-tie-loss}, Figure~\ref{fig:cd-crps}) support this distinction: QRF and DRF have statistically supported CRPS advantages over BDF, while BDF remains near the CRPS frontier with a more explicit distributional modeling interface.
The per-dataset comparison sharpens the interpretation (Table~\ref{tab:crps-per-dataset}). BDF's clear CRPS win over QRF occurs on \texttt{parkinsons\_updrs}, where the selected BDF variant uses Student-$t$ leaves (Table~\ref{tab:bdf-selection}); this is consistent with a local heavy-tailed likelihood helping when a parametric tail model is useful. On most datasets, however, QRF's purely empirical forest-induced predictive distribution has the edge, which explains its aggregate CRPS/CRPSS lead. The value proposition is therefore not that BDF replaces nonparametric forests on pure predictive score, but that it is the strongest parametric probabilistic competitor in this benchmark while retaining an explicit likelihood interface for density evaluation, tail assumptions, count/binary targets, and domain-constrained predictive distributions.

\paragraph{Which leaf family and split score are actually selected?}
Table~\ref{tab:bdf-selection} reports the BDF leaf family and split-score variant chosen by fold-0 tuning CRPS on each regression dataset. The headline pattern is that \emph{non-conjugate Student-$t$ leaves are dominant in regression}: 12 of 15 datasets select a Student-$t$ leaf family, while only 3 select the conjugate Normal--Normal leaf. Within the selected configurations, exact NLE is the split score on only 2 of 15 datasets (both Normal--Normal); NLL with the Laplace/BIC surrogate is selected on 3 of 15; and pure plug-in NLL is selected on the remaining 10. This picture is what justifies framing BDF as an \emph{evidence-centered forest architecture} rather than a strictly conjugate Bayesian method: exact NLE and the BIC evidence surrogate are principled split scores when they apply, while pure plug-in NLL remains a pragmatic log-score variant that is regularized by the architecture's tempered MDL-style split penalty in Eq.~\eqref{eq:net_gain}, the minimum-leaf-size constraint, ensembling, and distribution selection. Table~\ref{tab:bdf-conjugate-vs-selected-gap} quantifies the gap between the conjugate Normal--Normal variant (where NLE is available) and the actually-selected variant: the median per-dataset CRPS gap is $+1.82\%$, the mean is $+6.15\%$, and Normal--Normal matches or beats the selected variant on 4 of 15 datasets, with most of the larger gaps concentrated on heavy-tailed targets such as \texttt{parkinsons\_updrs} ($+46.87\%$) and \texttt{bike\_sharing} ($+20.29\%$) where the Student-$t$ leaf family is the substantive improvement rather than the split score. The focused Student-$t$ stability table (Table~\ref{tab:bdf-student-t-stability}) separates the two non-conjugate score choices: fold-0 tuning selects NLL+BIC on 3 of the 12 Student-$t$ selections and pure NLL on the remaining 9, with a median Normal--Normal-to-selected CRPS gap of $+2.41\%$. This is stability/sensitivity evidence for the approximation story, not a claim of exact Student-$t$ evidence validation. Classification is the inverse regime: all twelve classification datasets use the conjugate Beta--Bernoulli leaf, and exact NLE is selected on 8 of 12, with NLL on the remaining 4, which is consistent with conjugate Bayesian split scoring being the dominant configuration when the likelihood directly matches the target.

Figure~\ref{fig:coverage-vs-interval-score-rank} positions each method by its empirical coverage against the average interval-score rank. BDF attains the best average 90\% interval-score rank while keeping coverage close to nominal, slightly on the conservative side; QRF is similarly well calibrated and stronger on CRPS. Point-accurate competitors such as CatBoost-with-Uncertainty and NGBoost fall substantially below nominal coverage, reflecting the standard sharpness--calibration tradeoff. Conformalized forests achieve nominal coverage but at the cost of CRPS and interval score. PIT histograms and the conditional diagnostics in Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results} corroborate this picture without exposing a QRF failure mode: BDF and QRF are both stable and slightly conservative, while conformalized LightGBM preserves coverage only with substantially worse interval scores.

\paragraph{Point accuracy.}
BDF is not optimized for point prediction; on RMSE it trails dedicated point-accuracy methods such as CatBoost-with-Uncertainty and conformalized LightGBM. We view this as the expected cost of a distributional objective rather than a failure mode; the full RMSE comparison and scatterplots are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results} (Figures~\ref{fig:rmse-rel-to-best}, \ref{fig:regression-scatter}).

\subsection{Synthetic data-generating processes}\label{subsec:exp_synthetic}

We construct five synthetic DGPs---\emph{heteroscedastic sinusoidal}, \emph{bimodal mixture}, \emph{heavy-tailed}, \emph{step function}, and \emph{sparse sampling}---chosen to isolate shape phenomena (heteroscedasticity, multimodality, heavy tails, discontinuities, local data sparsity) that stress different components of a probabilistic predictor. Full DGP definitions are given in Appendix~\ref{app:experimental-setup}.

% HEADLINE PLOT: predictions grid across all DGPs and models.
\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{plots/synthetic/summary/predictions_grid.pdf}
    \caption{Predicted conditional distributions across DGPs (rows) and methods (columns) on a representative fold. Shaded bands show 90\% central predictive intervals; solid lines show predictive means.}
    \label{fig:synthetic-predictions-grid}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:synthetic-predictions-grid} compares predicted conditional distributions across DGPs and methods, including the nonparametric forest baselines QRF and DRF. The main synthetic conclusion is not that BDF uniformly wins; rather, the strong forest baselines are genuinely competitive and sometimes best. On the \emph{bimodal mixture} DGP, BDF-KDE recovers the two-mode shape among the explicit predictive-distribution models, but DRF attains the lowest CRPS and QRF remains close. On the \emph{step function} DGP, BDF produces sharp transitions aligned with the discontinuities---a direct consequence of evidence-based splitting, which accepts partitions whenever the log Bayes factor or evidence surrogate exceeds the depth/multiplicity threshold (Section~\ref{subsec:theory_split_bf})---while QRF is marginally best by CRPS and the remaining forest baselines are close.

The remaining DGPs show a similar tradeoff. On the \emph{heteroscedastic sinusoidal}, \emph{heavy-tailed}, and \emph{sparse sampling} DGPs, BDF variants stay near the CRPS frontier and maintain near-nominal coverage, but CatBoost-UQ or DRF can be slightly better by CRPS depending on the regime. Thus the synthetic study supports the revised positioning of BDF: it provides explicit parametric or KDE predictive distributions with competitive calibration and shape recovery, while QRF/DRF remain strong nonparametric forest baselines that can match or exceed BDF on predictive scores. Aggregate calibration curves, metric tables, per-DGP spread--skill plots, conditional calibration heatmaps, and PIT histograms are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:synthetic-diagnostics}.

\subsection{Split-scoring criterion: NLE vs.\ BIC}\label{subsec:exp_scoring}

A core design choice in BDF is the use of the negative log evidence (NLE) for conjugate leaves, the Laplace/BIC surrogate as a principled evidence approximation when closed-form evidence is unavailable, and pure plug-in NLL as a pragmatic log-score variant that is regularized by the rest of the architecture (Section~\ref{subsec:split_scoring}). We separate three empirical questions: (i) how close the scores are on a conjugate leaf where NLE is the principled reference, (ii) which score is selected in practice on real data when alternatives are available to tuning, and (iii) whether the non-conjugate Student-$t$ selections are stable enough to support the approximation story. For (i), Table~\ref{tab:a2_real_data} compares NLE, NLL, NLL+AIC, and NLL+BIC on Normal--Normal leaves at matched hyperparameters: the four scores agree to within $\sim 10^{-3}$ in skill score on every dataset tested, with NLL+BIC slightly ahead in the small-$n$ regime. For (ii), Table~\ref{tab:bdf-selection} shows that on the 15 regression datasets exact NLE is selected on only 2, NLL+BIC on 3, and pure plug-in NLL on the remaining 10, while classification (where the Beta--Bernoulli leaf is conjugate and the likelihood directly matches the target) selects exact NLE on 8 of 12 datasets. For (iii), Table~\ref{tab:bdf-student-t-stability} reports the selected Student-$t$ score choices and their held-out CRPS gaps against the conjugate reference; because exact Student-$t$ evidence is unavailable in the implementation, we treat this as stability/sensitivity evidence rather than as a direct approximation-error measurement against a slower exact reference. The broader predictive-score alternatives discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:split_scoring} are not all part of the headline benchmark: AIC appears in the scoring-method appendix, while LOO/$K$-fold corrections are used only in KDE and robustness-oriented configurations where the implementation cost is acceptable.

The empirical pattern matches the theory in Section~\ref{subsec:theory_surrogates}: NLE and BIC converge in ranking and acceptance behavior as leaf sample size grows, and their gap is concentrated in the small-$n_\ell$ regime where the $O(1)$ Laplace remainder in~\eqref{eq:laplace_bic_theory} is numerically comparable to the split gain. In this regime NLE benefits from exact posterior shrinkage via the Occam factor~\eqref{eq:evidence_decomposition}, whereas BIC applies a fixed $\tfrac{k}{2}\log n_\ell$ penalty and can be more robust when the prior is imperfectly matched. The practical implication is that conjugate families should use NLE when closed-form evidence is available and well calibrated, NLL+BIC is the principled default for non-conjugate or plug-in leaves, and pure plug-in NLL can be competitive in the low-bias forest regime when the leaf family is well chosen and the tempered MDL-style penalty in~\eqref{eq:net_gain}, the minimum-leaf-size constraint, and ensembling absorb the missing complexity term. This is the intended interpretation of the plug-in selections: they are useful heuristics inside a regularized probabilistic forest, not evidence that the selected non-conjugate variant is fully Bayesian. Interaction plots, CD diagrams, robustness splits, and real-data comparisons are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:scoring-method-full}.

Figure~\ref{fig:complexity-scaling} reports wall-clock fit time as a function of dataset size for BDF and a random-forest reference implementation; Table~\ref{tab:complexity} reports the corresponding empirical scaling exponents and overhead ratios. Three design choices explain this profile: (i) sufficient-statistic leaf updates are $O(1)$ per observation for conjugate leaves (Appendix~\ref{app:conjugate_updates}); (ii) the fixed-resolution candidate cutpoint grid bounds the split search at each node by $K \cdot \lfloor 1/\eta\rfloor$, independent of $n$ (Section~\ref{subsec:complexity_growth}); and (iii) trees are trained independently and admit trivial parallelization over the ensemble.
