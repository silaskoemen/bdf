\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}

This section evaluates Bayesian Distributional Forests (BDF) against a suite of probabilistic tabular baselines on four axes: (i) real-data benchmarks covering regression and classification (Section~\ref{subsec:exp_real}), (ii) controlled synthetic data-generating processes that probe distributional shape recovery (Section~\ref{subsec:exp_synthetic}), (iii) the choice of split-scoring criterion (Section~\ref{subsec:exp_scoring}), and (iv) computational complexity (Section~\ref{subsec:exp_complexity}). Additional ablations---conditional calibration by predicted uncertainty, parameter sensitivity, misspecification, noise features, convergence behavior, ensemble size, and conformalization comparisons---are deferred to Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results}.

% ==========================================
% 5.1 REAL-DATA BENCHMARKS
% ==========================================
\subsection{Real-data benchmarks}\label{subsec:exp_real}

\paragraph{Protocol.}
We evaluate on a collection of tabular regression and classification datasets sourced from UCI and OpenML; Appendix~\ref{app:experimental-setup} documents the full list, preprocessing, hyperparameter grids, and tuning protocol. All methods are tuned with the same compute budget, and every result is aggregated over multiple folds with standard errors reported in the appendix.

\subsubsection{Regression}\label{subsubsec:exp_regression}

Figure~\ref{fig:crps-rel-to-best} and Table~\ref{tab:rel-to-best-crps} summarize probabilistic accuracy via CRPS relative to the per-dataset best model. QRF is the strongest aggregate CRPS/CRPSS method, with average relative CRPS $1.251$ and average CRPSS rank $1.87$ (Table~\ref{tab:rankings-crpss}). BDF is the strongest parametric probabilistic model and the closest aggregate competitor by relative CRPS ($1.371$), ahead of conformalized LightGBM ($1.415$), CatBoost-with-Uncertainty ($1.428$), and the remaining baselines. The per-dataset sign test and critical-difference diagram (Table~\ref{tab:win-tie-loss}, Figure~\ref{fig:cd-crps}) support this distinction: QRF has a statistically supported CRPS advantage, while BDF remains near the CRPS frontier with a more explicit distributional modeling interface.
The per-dataset comparison sharpens the interpretation (Table~\ref{tab:crps-per-dataset}). BDF's clear CRPS win over QRF occurs on \texttt{parkinsons\_updrs}, where the selected BDF variant uses Student-$t$ leaves (Table~\ref{tab:bdf-selection}); this is consistent with the evidence-based split criterion and leaf-family correction helping when a local parametric tail model is useful. On most datasets, however, QRF's purely empirical forest-induced predictive distribution has the edge, which explains its aggregate CRPS/CRPSS lead.

    \caption{Empirical coverage at the 50/90/95\% levels versus average interval-score rank. BDF achieves nominal-to-slightly-conservative coverage at the lowest interval-score rank.}

Figure~\ref{fig:coverage-vs-interval-score-rank} positions each method by its empirical coverage against the average interval-score rank. BDF attains the best average 90\% interval-score rank while keeping coverage close to nominal, slightly on the conservative side; QRF is similarly well calibrated and stronger on CRPS. Point-accurate competitors such as CatBoost-with-Uncertainty and NGBoost fall substantially below nominal coverage, reflecting the standard sharpness--calibration tradeoff. Conformalized forests achieve nominal coverage but at the cost of CRPS and interval score. PIT histograms and the conditional diagnostics in Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results} corroborate this picture without exposing a QRF failure mode: BDF and QRF are both stable and slightly conservative, while conformalized LightGBM preserves coverage only with substantially worse interval scores.

\paragraph{Point accuracy.}
BDF is not optimized for point prediction; on RMSE it trails dedicated point-accuracy methods such as CatBoost-with-Uncertainty and conformalized LightGBM. We view this as the expected cost of a distributional objective rather than a failure mode; the full RMSE comparison and scatterplots are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results} (Figures~\ref{fig:rmse-rel-to-best}, \ref{fig:regression-scatter}).

Figure~\ref{fig:classification-rel-to-best-log-loss} and Table~\ref{tab:rel-to-best-log-loss} report log-loss relative to the per-dataset best classifier. LightGBM has the lowest average relative log-loss ($1.058$), while BDF and NGBoost are tied behind it at $1.075$; by average log-loss rank, BDF is first ($2.25$), followed by LightGBM ($2.50$) and RF ($3.00$) (Table~\ref{tab:rankings-log-loss}). Holm-corrected paired tests show a significant BDF advantage over KNN, but not over RF, LightGBM, NGBoost, or calibrated RF, so the strongest-baseline gaps should be read descriptively rather than as significant wins. Because BDF outputs posterior predictive probabilities rather than plug-in scores, log-loss is the natural primary metric; the Brier score provides a complementary quadratic-loss view and places BDF second behind LightGBM by average rank (Table~\ref{tab:rankings-brier}).
The reliability diagram (Figure~\ref{fig:classification-reliability}) shows BDF predictions aligned closely with the identity line across probability bins, with only minor deviations at the extremes. This calibrated behavior mirrors the regression coverage results: by integrating out leaf parameter uncertainty, BDF avoids severe overconfidence while remaining competitive with calibrated tree and boosting baselines. Per-dataset scatter plots, CD diagrams on log-loss and Brier score, and ECE statistics are deferred to Appendix~\ref{app:classification-additional} (Figures~\ref{fig:classification-cd}--\ref{fig:classification-scatter}).

\subsection{Synthetic data-generating processes}\label{subsec:exp_synthetic}

We construct five synthetic DGPs---\emph{heteroscedastic sinusoidal}, \emph{bimodal mixture}, \emph{heavy-tailed}, \emph{step function}, and \emph{sparse sampling}---chosen to isolate shape phenomena (heteroscedasticity, multimodality, heavy tails, discontinuities, local data sparsity) that stress different components of a probabilistic predictor. Full DGP definitions are given in Appendix~\ref{app:experimental-setup}.

% HEADLINE PLOT: predictions grid across all DGPs and models.
\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{plots/synthetic/summary/predictions_grid.pdf}
    \caption{Predicted conditional distributions across DGPs (rows) and methods (columns) on a representative fold. Shaded bands show 90\% central predictive intervals; solid lines show predictive means.}
    \label{fig:synthetic-predictions-grid}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:synthetic-predictions-grid} compares predicted conditional distributions across DGPs and methods. Several patterns emerge. On the \emph{bimodal mixture} DGP, only BDF recovers a visibly bimodal predictive density: the forest mixture~\eqref{eq:forest_mixture} over randomized partitions assigns mass to both modes whenever different trees isolate different component regions, whereas unimodal baselines (NGBoost, CatBoost-UQ) collapse to the mean of the mixture and underestimate tail mass. On the \emph{step function} DGP, BDF produces sharp transitions aligned with the discontinuities---a direct consequence of evidence-based splitting, which accepts partitions whenever the log Bayes factor exceeds the depth/multiplicity threshold (Section~\ref{subsec:theory_split_bf})---while smooth methods (Gaussian process baselines, Bayesian ridge) spread uncertainty across the discontinuity.

On the \emph{heavy-tailed} DGP, BDF with Student-$t$ leaves attains near-nominal coverage with tighter interval width than Gaussian-leaf variants, confirming that leaf-family choice controls tail behavior without requiring global distributional assumptions. The \emph{heteroscedastic sinusoidal} and \emph{sparse sampling} DGPs stress local variance estimation: the per-leaf posterior on the scale parameter inflates predictive width where data are sparse, as desired, while contracting it where they are dense. Aggregate calibration curves, metric tables, per-DGP spread--skill plots, conditional calibration heatmaps, and PIT histograms are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:synthetic-diagnostics}.

\subsection{Split-scoring criterion: NLE vs.\ BIC}\label{subsec:exp_scoring}

A core design choice in BDF is the use of the negative log evidence (NLE) for conjugate leaves and its BIC surrogate otherwise (Section~\ref{subsec:split_scoring}). We verify empirically that (i) NLE and BIC agree asymptotically on conjugate families, and (ii) NLE provides a non-trivial advantage in the small-leaf regime where BIC's $O(1)$ remainder is non-negligible.

The empirical pattern matches the theory in Section~\ref{subsec:theory_surrogates}: NLE and BIC converge in ranking and acceptance behavior as leaf sample size grows, and their gap is concentrated in the small-$n_\ell$ regime where the $O(1)$ Laplace remainder in~\eqref{eq:laplace_bic_theory} is numerically comparable to the split gain. In this regime NLE benefits from exact posterior shrinkage via the Occam factor~\eqref{eq:evidence_decomposition}, whereas BIC applies a fixed $\tfrac{k}{2}\log n_\ell$ penalty. The practical implication is that conjugate families should use NLE whenever closed-form evidence is available, while non-conjugate leaves may rely on BIC with minimal loss at moderate $n_\ell$. Interaction plots, CD diagrams, robustness splits, and real-data comparisons are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:scoring-method-full}.

Figure~\ref{fig:complexity-scaling} reports wall-clock fit time as a function of dataset size. BDF scales comparably to LightGBM and is tens of times faster than NGBoost, BART, and CatBoost-UQ on the same hardware and hyperparameter budget. Three design choices explain this profile: (i) sufficient-statistic leaf updates are $O(1)$ per observation (Appendix~\ref{app:conjugate_updates}); (ii) the fixed-resolution candidate cutpoint grid bounds the split search at each node by $K \cdot \lfloor 1/\eta\rfloor$, independent of $n$ (Section~\ref{subsec:complexity_growth}); and (iii) trees are trained independently and admit trivial parallelization over the ensemble. Memory usage and the overhead ratio relative to a non-probabilistic GBDT baseline are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:complexity-additional} (Figure~\ref{fig:complexity-extras}).
