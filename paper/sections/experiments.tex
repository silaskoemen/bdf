\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}

This section evaluates Bayesian Distributional Forests (BDF) against a suite of probabilistic tabular baselines on four axes: (i) real-data benchmarks covering regression and classification (Section~\ref{subsec:exp_real}), (ii) controlled synthetic data-generating processes that probe distributional shape recovery (Section~\ref{subsec:exp_synthetic}), (iii) the choice of split-scoring criterion (Section~\ref{subsec:exp_scoring}), and (iv) computational complexity (Section~\ref{subsec:exp_complexity}). Additional ablations---conditional calibration by predicted uncertainty, parameter sensitivity, misspecification, noise features, convergence behavior, ensemble size, and conformalization comparisons---are deferred to Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results}.

% ==========================================
% 5.1 REAL-DATA BENCHMARKS
% ==========================================
\subsection{Real-data benchmarks}\label{subsec:exp_real}

\paragraph{Protocol.}
We evaluate on a collection of tabular regression and classification datasets sourced from UCI and OpenML; Appendix~\ref{app:experimental-setup} documents the full list, preprocessing, hyperparameter grids, and tuning protocol. All methods are tuned with the same compute budget, and every result is aggregated over multiple folds with standard errors reported in the appendix. The numerical settings called \texttt{tuning\_sample\_size}=100 and \texttt{sample\_size}=1000 refer to predictive draws or quantile grid evaluations used for CRPS and interval metrics, not to the number of training or test observations.
The main real-data tables emphasize probabilistic tree, boosting, Bayesian, conformal, QRF/DRF, and XGBoostLSS baselines with stable benchmark implementations; Appendix~\ref{app:experimental-setup-models} records the exact model set and implementation caveats.

\subsubsection{Regression}\label{subsubsec:exp_regression}

Figure~\ref{fig:crps-rel-to-best} and Table~\ref{tab:rel-to-best-crps} summarize probabilistic accuracy via CRPS relative to the per-dataset best model. QRF is the strongest aggregate CRPS/CRPSS method, with average relative CRPS $1.270$ and average CRPSS rank $2.13$ (Table~\ref{tab:rankings-crpss}); DRF is second by both summaries. BDF is the strongest parametric probabilistic model by relative CRPS ($1.391$), ahead of conformalized LightGBM ($1.432$), CatBoost-with-Uncertainty ($1.444$), XGBoostLSS ($3.084$), and the remaining baselines. The per-dataset sign test and critical-difference diagram (Table~\ref{tab:win-tie-loss}, Figure~\ref{fig:cd-crps}) support this distinction: QRF and DRF have statistically supported CRPS advantages over BDF, while BDF remains near the CRPS frontier with a more explicit distributional modeling interface.
The per-dataset comparison sharpens the interpretation (Table~\ref{tab:crps-per-dataset}). BDF's clear CRPS win over QRF occurs on \texttt{parkinsons\_updrs}, where the selected BDF variant uses Student-$t$ leaves (Table~\ref{tab:bdf-selection}); this is consistent with the NLL+BIC evidence surrogate and local heavy-tailed likelihood helping when a parametric tail model is useful. On most datasets, however, QRF's purely empirical forest-induced predictive distribution has the edge, which explains its aggregate CRPS/CRPSS lead.

Figure~\ref{fig:coverage-vs-interval-score-rank} positions each method by its empirical coverage against the average interval-score rank. BDF attains the best average 90\% interval-score rank while keeping coverage close to nominal, slightly on the conservative side; QRF is similarly well calibrated and stronger on CRPS. Point-accurate competitors such as CatBoost-with-Uncertainty and NGBoost fall substantially below nominal coverage, reflecting the standard sharpness--calibration tradeoff. Conformalized forests achieve nominal coverage but at the cost of CRPS and interval score. PIT histograms and the conditional diagnostics in Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results} corroborate this picture without exposing a QRF failure mode: BDF and QRF are both stable and slightly conservative, while conformalized LightGBM preserves coverage only with substantially worse interval scores.

\paragraph{Point accuracy.}
BDF is not optimized for point prediction; on RMSE it trails dedicated point-accuracy methods such as CatBoost-with-Uncertainty and conformalized LightGBM. We view this as the expected cost of a distributional objective rather than a failure mode; the full RMSE comparison and scatterplots are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:additional-results} (Figures~\ref{fig:rmse-rel-to-best}, \ref{fig:regression-scatter}).

LightGBM has the lowest average relative log-loss ($1.058$), while BDF and NGBoost are tied behind it at $1.075$; by average log-loss rank, BDF is first ($2.25$), followed by LightGBM ($2.50$) and RF ($3.00$) (Table~\ref{tab:rankings-log-loss}). Holm-corrected paired tests show a significant BDF advantage over KNN, but not over RF, LightGBM, NGBoost, or calibrated RF, so the strongest-baseline gaps should be read descriptively rather than as significant wins. Because BDF outputs posterior predictive probabilities rather than plug-in scores, log-loss is the natural primary metric; the Brier score provides a complementary quadratic-loss view and places BDF second behind LightGBM by average rank (Table~\ref{tab:rankings-brier}).
The reliability diagram (Figure~\ref{fig:classification-reliability}) shows BDF predictions aligned closely with the identity line across probability bins, with only minor deviations at the extremes. This calibrated behavior mirrors the regression coverage results: Beta--Bernoulli leaf uncertainty helps avoid severe overconfidence while remaining competitive with calibrated tree and boosting baselines. \subsection{Synthetic data-generating processes}\label{subsec:exp_synthetic}

We construct five synthetic DGPs---\emph{heteroscedastic sinusoidal}, \emph{bimodal mixture}, \emph{heavy-tailed}, \emph{step function}, and \emph{sparse sampling}---chosen to isolate shape phenomena (heteroscedasticity, multimodality, heavy tails, discontinuities, local data sparsity) that stress different components of a probabilistic predictor. Full DGP definitions are given in Appendix~\ref{app:experimental-setup}.

% HEADLINE PLOT: predictions grid across all DGPs and models.
\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{plots/synthetic/summary/predictions_grid.pdf}
    \caption{Predicted conditional distributions across DGPs (rows) and methods (columns) on a representative fold. Shaded bands show 90\% central predictive intervals; solid lines show predictive means.}
    \label{fig:synthetic-predictions-grid}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:synthetic-predictions-grid} compares predicted conditional distributions across DGPs and methods. Several patterns emerge. On the \emph{bimodal mixture} DGP, BDF recovers a visibly bimodal predictive density among the plotted parametric and boosting baselines: the forest mixture~\eqref{eq:forest_mixture} over randomized partitions assigns mass to both modes whenever different trees isolate different component regions, whereas unimodal baselines (NGBoost, CatBoost-UQ) collapse to the mean of the mixture and underestimate tail mass. We add QRF to this synthetic comparison to test whether the effect is specific to BDF or shared by empirical forest-neighborhood methods. On the \emph{step function} DGP, BDF produces sharp transitions aligned with the discontinuities---a direct consequence of evidence-based splitting, which accepts partitions whenever the log Bayes factor or evidence surrogate exceeds the depth/multiplicity threshold (Section~\ref{subsec:theory_split_bf})---while smooth methods (Gaussian process baselines, Bayesian ridge) spread uncertainty across the discontinuity.

On the \emph{heavy-tailed} DGP, BDF with Student-$t$ leaves attains near-nominal coverage with tighter interval width than Gaussian-leaf variants, confirming that leaf-family choice controls tail behavior without requiring global distributional assumptions. The \emph{heteroscedastic sinusoidal} and \emph{sparse sampling} DGPs stress local variance estimation: conjugate variance or scale-aware leaves inflate predictive width where data are sparse while contracting it where they are dense. Aggregate calibration curves, metric tables, per-DGP spread--skill plots, conditional calibration heatmaps, and PIT histograms are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:synthetic-diagnostics}.

\subsection{Split-scoring criterion: NLE vs.\ BIC}\label{subsec:exp_scoring}

A core design choice in BDF is the use of the negative log evidence (NLE) for conjugate leaves and likelihood-based surrogates such as NLL+BIC otherwise (Section~\ref{subsec:split_scoring}). We verify empirically that NLE and BIC agree asymptotically on conjugate families, while finite-sample rankings can favor either exact NLE or NLL+BIC depending on the DGP, leaf family, and minimum-leaf-size regime. The broader predictive-score alternatives discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:split_scoring} are not all part of the headline benchmark: AIC appears in the scoring-method appendix, while LOO/$K$-fold corrections are used only in KDE and robustness-oriented configurations where the implementation cost is acceptable.

The empirical pattern matches the theory in Section~\ref{subsec:theory_surrogates}: NLE and BIC converge in ranking and acceptance behavior as leaf sample size grows, and their gap is concentrated in the small-$n_\ell$ regime where the $O(1)$ Laplace remainder in~\eqref{eq:laplace_bic_theory} is numerically comparable to the split gain. In this regime NLE benefits from exact posterior shrinkage via the Occam factor~\eqref{eq:evidence_decomposition}, whereas BIC applies a fixed $\tfrac{k}{2}\log n_\ell$ penalty and can be more robust when the prior is imperfectly matched. The practical implication is that conjugate families should use NLE when closed-form evidence is available and well calibrated, while NLL+BIC is a strong default for plug-in or non-conjugate leaves. Interaction plots, CD diagrams, robustness splits, and real-data comparisons are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:scoring-method-full}.

Figure~\ref{fig:complexity-scaling} reports wall-clock fit time as a function of dataset size for BDF and a random-forest reference implementation; Table~\ref{tab:complexity} reports the corresponding empirical scaling exponents and overhead ratios. Three design choices explain this profile: (i) sufficient-statistic leaf updates are $O(1)$ per observation for conjugate leaves (Appendix~\ref{app:conjugate_updates}); (ii) the fixed-resolution candidate cutpoint grid bounds the split search at each node by $K \cdot \lfloor 1/\eta\rfloor$, independent of $n$ (Section~\ref{subsec:complexity_growth}); and (iii) trees are trained independently and admit trivial parallelization over the ensemble. Memory usage and the overhead ratio relative to the same random-forest reference are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:complexity-additional} (Figure~\ref{fig:complexity-extras}).

These experiments confirm the qualitative findings above and characterize the regions of parameter and data space where BDF's advantages are largest.
