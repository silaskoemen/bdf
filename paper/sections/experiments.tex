\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}

This section evaluates BDF on real-data regression and classification benchmarks, controlled synthetic DGPs, split-scoring ablations, and computational complexity. Full real-data tables are in Appendix~\ref{app:real-data-results}; synthetic and robustness diagnostics are in Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostics-robustness}.

% ==========================================
% 5.1 REAL-DATA BENCHMARKS
% ==========================================
\subsection{Real-data benchmarks}\label{subsec:exp_real}

\paragraph{Protocol.}
We evaluate on UCI and OpenML tabular datasets; Appendix~\ref{app:experimental-setup} documents preprocessing (one-hot categorical encoding and missing-row removal, applied identically to all methods), model grids, tuning, and fold protocol. Every tunable model configuration receives 50 Optuna trials on the same processed numeric matrices, and reported summaries aggregate folds 1--9 after fold-0 selection. For regression, BDF-Full selects on fold 0 among four canonical leaf-family configurations, each of which tunes its eligible score regimes internally. The separately run fixed-score BDF configurations appear only in ablation tables and do not enlarge the BDF-Full selection pool. XGBoostLSS is analogously fused over four distributional configurations, and NGBoost over Normal, Laplace, LogNormal, Exponential, and Poisson configurations wherever target support permits. A candidate distribution must produce nine finite CRPS and RMSE evaluation folds to remain eligible; incomplete or non-finite candidate/dataset pairs are treated as numerical failures and excluded rather than imputed, while finite but arbitrarily poor scores are retained. The tuning budget is therefore equal per configuration rather than per aggregated method family.
The regression comparison includes QRF and DRF as strong nonparametric forest baselines; the classification comparison focuses on probabilistic classifiers with predicted class probabilities.
Because BDF-Full, XGBoostLSS, and NGBoost are validation-selected aggregates rather than single estimators, Table~\ref{tab:budget-summary} makes each method's total tuning budget explicit; the equal-per-configuration budget means aggregated families receive more total trials than single-configuration baselines, which is the deliberate design choice discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:benchmark-protocol}.

We organize the empirical analysis around three questions: whether exact evidence helps when it is available, whether validation-selected leaf families and score regimes add value beyond a fixed evidence reference, and whether the resulting BDF-Full framework is competitive with strong distributional baselines.

\subsubsection{Regression}\label{subsubsec:exp_regression}

\paragraph{Question 1: Does exact evidence help?}
Table~\ref{tab:bdf-normal-nle-vs-full} gives the strict regression evidence comparison: a fixed Normal--Normal NLE Core reference versus BDF-Full and selected baselines. The exact-NLE reference is competitive but not dominant. Two mechanisms are consistent with this gap. First, real-valued targets rarely match one homoscedastic Gaussian leaf law exactly, unlike the Bernoulli outcome family in the classification comparison below. Second, the evidence Occam factor in Eq.~\eqref{eq:evidence_decomposition} requires each split to justify moving two child posteriors away from the prior; this can favor shallower trees in precisely the small-leaf regime where a forest can instead average the variance of deeper, potentially lower-bias plug-in trees. The real-data benchmark cannot separate outcome-family misspecification from this score-by-ensemble interaction, so we test both mechanisms directly in a controlled factorial experiment (Appendix~\ref{app:factorial-evidence}): with all other hyperparameters fixed, evidence scoring is best when the leaf family is well specified and no forest averages away plug-in variance, plug-in NLL is best under misspecification at scale, and ensembling compresses the difference---supporting selectable score regimes as a design principle rather than a tuning artifact.

\paragraph{Question 2: Does family and score selection help?}
Table~\ref{tab:bdf-selection} reports the BDF leaf family and split-score variant chosen by fold-0 CRPS. These counts are descriptive outcomes of the pre-specified tuning split rather than estimates of selection probabilities under repeated tuning splits. Regression selects Student-$t$ leaves on 10 of 15 datasets and Normal--Normal leaves on 5; the KDE and Gamma--Poisson candidates, although eligible wherever the target domain permits, are selected on 0 of 15. NLL+BIC is selected on 9 datasets and pure plug-in NLL on 6, with no exact-NLE selections, supporting the framing of BDF as a regularized log-scoring framework with optional exact-evidence leaves rather than a strictly conjugate-Bayesian regression method. On the 10 datasets where tuning preferred a different family, the tuned Normal--Normal variant costs a median $+2.26\%$ CRPS (worst case $+10.10\%$); the median over all 15 datasets is $+0.76\%$, but 5 of those select Normal--Normal itself and contribute a structural zero (Table~\ref{tab:bdf-conjugate-vs-selected-gap}). The fixed-NLE Normal--Normal Core reference in Table~\ref{tab:bdf-normal-nle-vs-full} gives the stricter exact-evidence comparison; its relative-CRPS column is normalized within that comparison set, unlike the full-benchmark relative CRPS in Table~\ref{tab:rel-to-best-crps}. Table~\ref{tab:bdf-student-t-stability} separates the two Student-$t$ score choices.

\paragraph{Question 3: Does BDF-Full beat strong distributional baselines?}
Figure~\ref{fig:crps-rel-to-best} and Table~\ref{tab:rel-to-best-crps} summarize CRPS relative to the per-dataset best model; per-dataset values are in Table~\ref{tab:crps-per-dataset}. QRF is the strongest aggregate CRPS/CRPSS method, with average relative CRPS $1.287$ and average CRPSS rank $2.13$ (Table~\ref{tab:rankings-crpss}); DRF is second by both summaries. BDF-Full is the strongest parametric probabilistic model by relative CRPS ($1.405$), ahead of conformalized LightGBM ($1.445$), CatBoost-with-Uncertainty ($1.455$), and XGBoostLSS ($3.323$). NGBoost ranks sixth by CRPSS, but its scale-sensitive average relative CRPS is dominated by a finite catastrophic Exponential fit on \texttt{forest\_fires}; we retain that result rather than clip it. The sign test (Table~\ref{tab:win-tie-loss}) shows a CRPS advantage for QRF over BDF-Full that survives Holm correction \citep{holm1979simple}, and DRF is ahead on 12 of 15 datasets (nominal $p=0.013$, not significant after Holm correction); the critical-difference diagram (Figure~\ref{fig:cd-crps}), constructed by the Friedman/Nemenyi protocol of \citet{demsar2006statistical}, gives the same ordering. BDF's value is therefore not universal CRPS dominance but an explicit likelihood interface near the probabilistic-score frontier.

Figure~\ref{fig:coverage-vs-interval-score-rank} positions empirical coverage against average interval-score rank. BDF-Full has the best average 90\% interval-score rank with close-to-nominal, slightly conservative coverage; QRF is similarly calibrated and stronger on CRPS. Point-accurate competitors such as CatBoost-with-Uncertainty and NGBoost under-cover, while conformalized forests recover coverage at the cost of CRPS and interval score.

\paragraph{Point accuracy.}
BDF is not optimized for point prediction, but average relative RMSE is informative: CatBoost-with-Uncertainty and conformalized LightGBM lead, while BDF-Full follows at $1.352$ and remains ahead of QRF ($1.421$) and DRF ($1.514$) (Figure~\ref{fig:rmse-rel-to-best}). BDF-Full is thus the only method in the comparison with lower average relative CRPS than every boosted-tree baseline \emph{and} lower average relative RMSE than the nonparametric CRPS leaders QRF and DRF: it trades the top of either single-metric ranking for the strongest joint sharpness--probabilistic-score profile in the benchmark.

Figure~\ref{fig:classification-rel-to-best-log-loss} and Table~\ref{tab:rel-to-best-log-loss} report log-loss relative to the per-dataset best classifier. LightGBM has the lowest average relative log-loss ($1.065$), followed by NGBoost ($1.081$) and BDF-Full ($1.083$); by average log-loss rank, BDF-Full is first ($2.75$), followed by LightGBM ($2.92$) and RF ($3.42$) (Table~\ref{tab:rankings-log-loss}). Holm-corrected \citep{holm1979simple} sign tests (Table~\ref{tab:classification-win-tie-loss}) show a significant BDF-Full advantage over KNN, but not over RF, LightGBM, NGBoost, or calibrated RF. Table~\ref{tab:bdf-core-vs-full-classification} shows that the fixed-NLE Beta--Bernoulli Core variant and tuned classification BDF-Full are nearly identical: the median log-loss gap is $+0.47\%$, and Core has similar ECE. The reliability diagram (Figure~\ref{fig:classification-reliability}) shows BDF-Full predictions closely aligned with the identity line, consistent with the calibrated behavior seen in regression.

Classification is the cleanest evidence case in the benchmark in a specific sense: a binary response is Bernoulli conditional on any leaf region, so the Beta--Bernoulli leaf removes response-support misspecification at the leaf level and admits closed-form evidence. This does not make the fitted model correctly specified---the partition approximation of $\mathbf{x}\mapsto P(Y{=}1\mid\mathbf{x})$, adaptive split selection, and probability heterogeneity within leaves remain the principal modelling errors. The near-equivalence of fixed-NLE BDF-Core and tuned BDF-Full therefore supports a correspondingly narrow claim: within the submitted tuning space and datasets, varying the Beta--Bernoulli score regime adds little once the outcome family is fixed correctly, whereas for regression targets that are heavy-tailed or otherwise misspecified under a single conjugate family, selecting among regularized fitted leaf laws is more useful.

\subsection{Synthetic data-generating processes}\label{subsec:exp_synthetic}

We construct five synthetic DGPs---\emph{heteroscedastic sinusoidal}, \emph{bimodal mixture}, \emph{heavy-tailed}, \emph{step function}, and \emph{sparse sampling}---chosen to isolate shape phenomena (heteroscedasticity, multimodality, heavy tails, discontinuities, local data sparsity) that stress different components of a probabilistic predictor. Full DGP definitions are given in Appendix~\ref{app:experimental-setup}.

\subsection{Split-scoring criterion: NLE vs.\ BIC}\label{subsec:exp_scoring}

The scoring ablation asks whether NLE, NLL+BIC, and pure NLL materially change the fitted predictor. At matched hyperparameters, Table~\ref{tab:a2_real_data} shows close agreement among the retained scores on conjugate leaves. On the full regression benchmark, however, fold-0 tuning often selects evidence surrogates or plug-in scores (Table~\ref{tab:bdf-selection}), especially with Student-$t$ leaves (Table~\ref{tab:bdf-student-t-stability}). Table~\ref{tab:bdf-unified-ablation} puts the fixed BDF score regimes, BDF-Full, and selected external baselines under one normalization, while Table~\ref{tab:bdf-leaf-score-ablation} isolates the BDF-only score effect. The Normal--Normal scores are close in aggregate CRPS, with plug-in NLL and NLL+BIC slightly ahead of fixed NLE, while Student-$t$ leaves are competitive under both pure NLL and NLL+BIC, with pure NLL giving the best average interval-score rank. We therefore treat pure NLL selections as useful regularized heuristics inside the full BDF architecture, not as evidence of exact Bayesian Student-$t$ inference.

Because the real-data comparison confounds score regime with leaf family, depth, and tuning, Appendix~\ref{app:factorial-evidence} isolates the score effect factorially on synthetic DGPs, crossing leaf specification, sample size, score regime, and single-tree versus forest prediction at fixed hyperparameters. The factorial results characterize when evidence helps: evidence and BIC scores grow markedly smaller trees and attain the best held-out log density when the leaf family is well specified and predictions come from a single tree, whereas pure plug-in NLL wins under leaf misspecification at larger sample sizes, and forest aggregation compresses the remaining score-regime differences to a few percent. This matches the dataset-dependent selections above and locates the regime---small samples, well-specified leaves, small ensembles---in which exact evidence is most valuable.

Figure~\ref{fig:complexity-scaling} reports wall-clock fit and prediction time as functions of sample size, feature count, and ensemble size for BDF and a random-forest reference implementation; Table~\ref{tab:complexity} reports the corresponding empirical scaling exponents and overhead ratios. Three design choices explain this profile: (i) sufficient-statistic leaf updates are $O(1)$ per observation for conjugate leaves (Appendix~\ref{app:conjugate_updates}); (ii) the fixed-resolution candidate cutpoint grid bounds the split search at each node by $K \cdot m_{\max}$, independent of $n$ (Section~\ref{subsec:complexity_growth}); and (iii) trees are trained independently and admit trivial parallelization over the ensemble. Prediction-time overhead reflects evaluation of full predictive distributions in a first implementation rather than a claim of optimized inference latency; ensemble size and implementation optimizations provide the natural speed--accuracy tradeoff. Memory usage and the overhead ratio relative to the same random-forest reference are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:complexity-additional} (Figure~\ref{fig:complexity-extras}).
