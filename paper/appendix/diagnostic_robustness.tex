\subsection{Local calibration by predicted uncertainty}\label{app:conditional-diagnostics}

Marginal coverage can hide failures that occur only in low- or high-uncertainty regions. To check whether the regression intervals remain calibrated locally, we recompute evaluation predictions using the stored best hyperparameters and bin test points by each method's predicted interquartile range (IQR). Within each decile we report empirical 90\% coverage, 90\% interval score, and CRPS. Table~\ref{tab:conditional-diagnostics} summarizes the per-decile deviations; Figure~\ref{fig:conditional-coverage-heatmap} shows the decile-level coverage errors and Figure~\ref{fig:conditional-interval-score} the corresponding interval scores. BDF and QRF combine near-nominal global coverage with small per-decile deviations (mean absolute deviation $\le 0.045$), ConfLGBM attains the smallest per-decile deviations at a higher interval score, and CatBoost-UQ's marginal under-coverage persists at the decile level (mean absolute deviation $0.179$, worst decile $-0.203$). We emphasize that binning on a model's own predicted IQR is one specific local-calibration diagnostic; it does not establish conditional calibration with respect to arbitrary covariate-dependent events.

\subsection{Factorial evidence study: when does integrated evidence help?}\label{app:factorial-evidence}

The real-data benchmark confounds the split-score regime with leaf family, tree depth, and per-dataset tuning, and the matched-hyperparameter check of Appendix~\ref{app:scoring-method-full} shows only that the regimes agree closely on real data, not when they should differ. This study isolates the score effect by crossing four factors on synthetic DGPs with known conditional laws: (i) leaf specification---a Normal leaf is correctly specified under Gaussian noise and misspecified under Student-$t_3$ noise, while a Student-$t$ leaf is correct under $t_3$ noise and over-flexible under Gaussian noise; (ii) training size $n\in\{300,3000\}$; (iii) score regime---exact/profile NLE, NLL+BIC, and pure plug-in NLL where available to the leaf family; and (iv) a deterministic single tree (no bootstrap or subsampling) versus a 50-tree randomized forest. The mean function is the heavy-tailed benchmark form $y = x_1^2 + x_2 + 0.5\,\varepsilon$, all non-score hyperparameters are fixed at the public estimator defaults with no per-cell tuning, and each cell averages 10 seeds. We report held-out CRPS from 1000 predictive samples and the \emph{exact} held-out negative log density of the uniform tree mixture, computed from the per-tree leaf parameters, together with the mean leaf count per tree (Table~\ref{tab:factorial-evidence}).

\input{../benchmarks/results/factorial_evidence/tables/factorial_evidence.tex}

Four patterns emerge. First, the evidence and BIC penalties act as tree-size control exactly as the Occam decomposition in Eq.~\eqref{eq:evidence_decomposition} predicts. At $n=3000$, NLE reduces the Normal single trees from 232--233 to 108--116 leaves and the corresponding forests from 174 to 103--111 leaves; NLL+BIC similarly reduces the Student-$t$ trees from 230--233 to 106--121 leaves.

Second, at $n=3000$, evidence-style scores deliver the best single-tree density forecasts in the correctly specified and over-flexible blocks with roughly half the leaves: NLE attains the best single-tree NLL on the correctly specified Normal block at $n=3000$ ($0.955$ versus $1.062$ for plug-in NLL), and NLL+BIC dominates the Student-$t$ single-tree blocks ($1.414$ versus $1.721$ at $n=3000$). These blocks illustrate how evidence regularization can improve density forecasts before ensembling.

Third, under leaf misspecification with sufficient data, pure plug-in NLL wins on both forest metrics (CRPS $0.426$ versus $0.432$/$0.443$; NLL $1.172$ versus $1.183$/$1.250$): deeper partitions reduce the outcome-family approximation bias that the evidence penalty suppresses. The same aggressive regime is unreliable at small $n$ without ensembling---the misspecified single-tree plug-in NLL reaches $2.454$ against $1.855$ for NLL+BIC---which is where the BIC-sized correction is most valuable.

Fourth, forest aggregation compresses the score-regime effect: the largest within-block NLL gap relative to the best score falls from about $32\%$ for single trees to $6.7\%$ for forests ($1.250$ versus $1.172$). CRPS gaps are at most about $4\%$, and 90\% coverage varies more with ensembling than with the score ($80.5\%$ mean coverage for single trees versus $91.8\%$ for forests, essentially identical across regimes).

Together these results help interpret the real-data selection pattern of Table~\ref{tab:bdf-selection}: evidence-style regularization can improve density forecasts with substantially fewer leaves, while ensembling often narrows its predictive difference from plug-in growth. The advantage is not uniform: at $n=300$, pure NLL wins the Normal/Gaussian block, whereas NLL+BIC improves the misspecified Normal single tree. Thus the absence of NLE selections in the real-data search does not imply a general predictive disadvantage for evidence scoring. This study also supplies the held-out log-density evaluation for explicit-density configurations under controlled DGPs that the sample-based real-data metrics do not cover.

\subsection{Likelihood misspecification}\label{app:misspecification}

We evaluate robustness of BDF when the assumed leaf likelihood does not match the data-generating distribution. Figure~\ref{fig:misspecification-degradation} summarizes CRPS degradation per leaf family as the DGP departs from the assumed likelihood, and Table~\ref{tab:misspecification-crps} gives the full comparison. Two observations bear directly on the leaf-selection results of Section~\ref{subsubsec:exp_regression}. First, misspecification costs are modest for the real-valued families: no real-valued leaf loses more than a few percent CRPS to the correctly specified family on any real-valued DGP. Second, the Student-$t$ leaf is within one standard error of the correctly specified (or best) family on every unimodal DGP, including the Gaussian and skewed cases where it is nominally misspecified, because its estimated degrees of freedom saturate toward the near-Gaussian regime when the leaf data show no excess kurtosis (Appendix~\ref{app:student_t_leaves}); only the multimodal DGP, where the KDE leaf is the appropriate choice, separates the families clearly. Its frequent selection in the real-data benchmark is therefore consistent with a robustness advantage at essentially no cost under correct specification, rather than an artifact of the tuning split.

\subsection{Noise features and multiplicity correction}\label{app:noise}

We study robustness to pure-noise features, which directly probe the tempered selection penalty $\gamma \log(K \cdot m_j)$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:net_gain}. Figure~\ref{fig:noise-summary} reports relative CRPS degradation and split-variable selection rates as up to 200 noise features are added; Table~\ref{tab:noise-features-crps} gives the absolute values. The relevant comparison is within-method degradation rather than absolute CRPS across methods (the boosted baselines are stronger on some of these synthetic DGPs outright): BDF's relative degradation remains comparable to conformal RF and NGBoost, and its split-selection rates continue to prefer signal features as the noise dimension grows, which is the behavior the multiplicity penalty is designed to protect.

\subsection{Convergence and learning curves}\label{app:convergence}

Figure~\ref{fig:convergence-rate} and Table~\ref{tab:convergence-rates} compare empirical convergence rates (slope of $\log$ MSE against $\log n$) for BDF and a random-forest reference across smooth, non-smooth, and mixed DGPs. In moderate dimensions ($p \in \{5,10\}$) BDF converges at least as fast as the reference on every DGP, with the largest advantages on non-smooth targets. The one-dimensional non-smooth DGPs reverse this ordering: with a single feature there is no multiplicity to penalize and axis-aligned splits are trivially aligned with the discontinuities, so the reference forest's aggressive splitting pays off while BDF's structural penalty slows refinement. Since $p=1$ is atypical for tabular applications, we read the table as favorable in the regime that matters and honest about the univariate edge case.

\subsection{Ensemble size}\label{app:ensemble-size}

Figure~\ref{fig:ensemble-combined} and Table~\ref{tab:n-trees-crps} track CRPS, coverage, and fit time as the number of trees $M$ grows. CRPS improvements drop below $1\%$ by $M=50$ on three of the four DGPs and by $M=100$ on the fourth, and fit time scales approximately linearly in $M$, so the benchmark default of tuning $M$ up to a few hundred trees is comfortably past the accuracy plateau; smaller ensembles trade little accuracy for proportional speedups at prediction time (Section~\ref{subsec:exp_complexity}).
