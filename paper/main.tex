\begin{abstract}%   <- trailing '%' for backward compatibility of .sty file
We introduce \emph{Bayesian Distributional Forests} (BDF), an evidence-centered probabilistic tree ensemble framework that performs Bayesian inference \emph{within leaves} when conjugate likelihood--prior pairs are available, and an evidence-regularized log-score procedure otherwise, while retaining greedy forest construction and randomized ensembling globally.
BDF trains trees by optimizing an additive \emph{log-score} objective over leaves: for conjugate likelihood--prior pairs this score is the \emph{negative log evidence} (a prequential Bayesian log-loss); for non-conjugate likelihoods it is the plug-in negative log-likelihood with a BIC-style complexity correction (a Laplace evidence surrogate for MLE/MAP fits, and a heuristic penalty for method-of-moments plug-in fits), or, in the architecture's pragmatic mode, the pure plug-in NLL whose missing complexity term is absorbed by the tempered MDL-style split penalty, the minimum-leaf-size constraint, and ensembling.
A global MDL-style penalty controls tree growth and complements Bayesian leaf uncertainty where available, while supplying architecture-level regularization for plug-in leaves.

BDF applies uniformly to regression and classification, supports weakly informative or empirical Bayes priors to stabilize small leaves, and admits fast training via closed-form sufficient-statistic updates and scalable split search.
Across diverse benchmark datasets, BDF is competitive with the strongest probabilistic baselines: quantile regression forests attain the lowest aggregate CRPS/CRPSS and DRF is also slightly ahead of BDF, while BDF is the strongest parametric probabilistic model by relative CRPS and has the best average 90\% interval-score rank with nominal or slightly conservative coverage. In binary classification, BDF attains the best average log-loss rank, trails LightGBM only slightly in relative log-loss, and remains statistically indistinguishable from the strongest tree and boosting baselines under Holm-corrected paired tests.
Overall, BDF brings Bayesian leaf principles into modern tree ensembles by making evidence-regularized split selection and probabilistic leaf mixtures computationally practical at scale, while making explicit where the implementation relies on principled heuristics rather than exact Bayes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Tree ensembles---Random Forests \citep{breiman2001random} and Gradient Boosted Trees \citep{friedman2001greedy,chen2016xgboost,ke2017lightgbm}---are a dominant paradigm for tabular prediction due to their accuracy, robustness, and favorable computational profile.
Their strength comes from learning data-adaptive partitions of the covariate space while retaining interpretability through leaf-level structure and post-hoc tools (e.g., feature importance, partial dependence).
However, most deployed tree implementations are optimized for \emph{point targets} (conditional means/medians or class labels) and provide uncertainty only indirectly (e.g., ensemble variance, quantile losses, post-hoc calibration).
In many applications---risk-sensitive decision making, selective prediction, monitoring, and downstream optimization---one instead requires a coherent estimate of the \emph{full conditional distribution} $p(y\mid \mathbf{x})$.

A growing literature equips trees with probabilistic outputs.
One direction fits \emph{distributional} models in terminal nodes.
Distributional regression forests \citep{schlosser2019distributional} combine recursive partitioning with parametric distributional models (in the spirit of GAMLSS), selecting splits using likelihood/score-based criteria and fitting leaf parameters by maximum likelihood.
A second direction uses \emph{probabilistic boosting}, where the parameters of a chosen output distribution are learned via gradient boosting; examples include NGBoost \citep{duan2020ngboost} and distributional boosting frameworks such as XGBoostLSS \citep{marz2019xgboostlss}.
These approaches provide expressive conditional distributions, but they typically remain \emph{frequentist at the leaf level}: uncertainty is represented through fitted distributional parameters, while parameter uncertainty is handled implicitly (or ignored), and split decisions rely on plug-in likelihood objectives or boosting dynamics.

At the other end of the spectrum, fully Bayesian tree models place priors over tree structures and leaf parameters and perform posterior inference \citep{denison1998bayesian,chipman2010bart}.
While conceptually appealing, such methods often rely on MCMC over tree structures, which can be substantially more expensive than modern split search and less straightforward to deploy at scale or with diverse likelihood families.
Recent work revisits Bayesian tree induction from a MAP perspective (e.g., MAPTree) \citep{sullivan2024maptree}, but a core tension remains: \emph{Bayesian structure inference} is expensive, whereas \emph{fast split optimization} is central to how trees are used in practice.

This paper targets the regime in which tree ensembles are most effective: scalable split search, many candidate partitions, and the need for uncertainty-aware prediction without full posterior sampling over tree structures.
We introduce \emph{Bayesian Distributional Forests} (BDF), which upgrade classical forests to probabilistic predictors by combining \emph{Bayesian inference within leaves when available} with evidence-regularized fitted leaves otherwise, and by selecting splits via \emph{log predictive scores}.

\paragraph{Key idea: Bayesian leaf principles + log-score-based split selection.}
BDF treats each leaf as a local probabilistic model. For conjugate leaves this is a Bayesian model with prior $\pi(\theta_\ell\mid\eta)$, posterior $\pi(\theta_\ell\mid\mathcal{D}_\ell,\eta)$, and posterior predictive $p_\ell(y\mid \mathcal{D}_\ell,\eta)$; for non-conjugate leaves it is an explicit fitted density scored by an evidence surrogate or by a regularized plug-in log score.
Tree induction is expressed through a summed leaf score $\sum_{\ell} S(\mathcal{D}_\ell)$, where $S$ is a negative log-score associated with the leaf predictor.
For conjugate likelihood--prior pairs, $S$ is the \emph{negative log evidence}, which admits a prequential interpretation as Bayesian cumulative log-loss.
When closed-form evidence is unavailable, the same objective is instantiated with a Laplace/BIC evidence surrogate, with a cross-validated log predictive density (LOO or $K$-fold), or---when the leaf family is well chosen and the architecture's other regularizers absorb the missing complexity term---with the plug-in negative log-likelihood directly. The experiments quantify how often each variant is selected by tuning in practice (Section~\ref{subsec:exp_real}, Table~\ref{tab:bdf-selection}).
A global MDL-style structural code controls tree growth: in the implementation used for all reported results, this code is the depth-dependent logistic prior plus the tempered split-identity penalty derived in Section~\ref{subsec:complexity_growth}.

\paragraph{Scalable probabilistic ensembles.}
BDF is not a posterior sampler over tree structures.
Instead, it combines Bayesian or evidence-regularized probabilistic leaves with standard randomized ensemble training (bagging and feature subsampling), producing predictions by mixing leaf-level predictive distributions across trees.
This retains the computational profile of classical forests while yielding coherent predictive distributions.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item We introduce Bayesian Distributional Forests, a scalable probabilistic tree ensemble framework built around Bayesian leaf inference where available and evidence-regularized probabilistic leaves more generally.
\item We formalize split selection through an additive leaf-wise log-score objective, and show how exact evidence (conjugate leaves), Laplace/BIC surrogates where the non-conjugate fit is MLE/MAP, BIC-style complexity corrections for faster plug-in leaves, and plug-in NLL (regularized by the architecture's MDL-style split penalty and minimum-leaf-size constraint) fit into a single training principle.
\item We provide a theoretical perspective connecting evidence-based training to prequential log scoring and the Occam effect in marginal likelihood, clarify when predictive surrogates are preferable (e.g., misspecification or conservative evidence), and report empirically which variant is selected by tuning on real data---the dominant configuration in regression uses non-conjugate Student-$t$ leaves with a plug-in or BIC-style score, while classification recovers conjugate Beta--Bernoulli leaves with exact NLE.
\item We empirically demonstrate that BDF is a competitive calibrated distributional predictor: QRF leads aggregate CRPS/CRPSS and DRF is also highly competitive, while BDF is the strongest parametric probabilistic model by relative CRPS, has the best average 90\% interval-score rank, achieves nominal coverage, and attains the best average log-loss rank in binary classification.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related-work}

BDF sits between scalable tree ensembles, distributional prediction, and Bayesian tree modeling. Random forests \citep{breiman2001random} and gradient-boosted trees \citep{friedman2001greedy,chen2016xgboost,ke2017lightgbm} are strong tabular predictors, but their native objectives typically target point functionals and leave uncertainty to post-hoc estimates, quantile losses, or calibration layers.

\paragraph{Distributional tree methods.}
Distributional regression forests \citep{schlosser2019distributional} fit distributional models in leaves and use likelihood/score-based splits; Distributional Random Forests \citep{cevid2022distributional} estimate conditional distributions through forest-induced weights. These methods produce useful probabilistic predictions, but they rely on plug-in or weighted-neighborhood estimates rather than leaf-level posteriors. BDF instead makes the leaf likelihood an explicit modeling object: it integrates over leaf-parameter uncertainty for conjugate leaves, uses evidence surrogates or regularized plug-in scores for non-conjugate leaves, and can use the evidence itself as a split score when it is available.
Mondrian forests and Mondrian-process tree priors \citep{lakshminarayanan2014mondrian} provide a related route to scalable online random partitions with uncertainty-aware predictions; BDF differs by using evidence-based greedy split selection and explicit leaf predictive distributions, with exact Bayesian posterior predictives in the conjugate cases.

\paragraph{Quantile and conformal prediction.}
Quantile regression forests \citep[QRF;][]{meinshausen2006quantile} estimate conditional quantiles from forest-induced neighborhoods and provide a strong nonparametric baseline for predictive intervals and CRPS. Conformal prediction \citep{vovk2005algorithmic,lei2018distribution,romano2019conformalized} gives distribution-free marginal coverage by calibrating residual or quantile scores on held-out data, and can wrap high-performing point or quantile predictors such as random forests and LightGBM. These methods are complementary to BDF: QRF avoids parametric leaf likelihoods but does not maintain Bayesian leaf posteriors, while conformal wrappers guarantee marginal coverage without directly modeling the full conditional density or leaf-parameter uncertainty.

\paragraph{Probabilistic boosting.}
NGBoost \citep{duan2020ngboost} and XGBoostLSS-style distributional boosting \citep{marz2019xgboostlss} learn distribution parameters with boosting dynamics and plug-in predictive likelihoods. Treeffuser \citep{beltranvelez2024treeffuser} uses tree-parameterized score models for conditional diffusion prediction. BDF is less globally expressive than diffusion-based density models, but it keeps the probabilistic model local and interpretable: distributional shape comes from explicit leaf likelihoods, Bayesian leaf updates where available, and mixtures over randomized partitions.

\paragraph{Bayesian tree models.}
Bayesian CART and BART \citep{denison1998bayesian,chipman2010bart} place priors over tree structures and leaf parameters and typically use MCMC over tree topologies. MAP-based Bayesian tree induction, such as MAPTree \citep{sullivan2024maptree}, improves scalability for single-tree structure inference. BDF is complementary: it does not sample or optimize a global posterior over forest structures, but adopts Bayesian inference where it is cheap and reusable---inside leaves---while retaining fast greedy split search and randomized ensembling.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:methodology}

Bayesian Distributional Forests (BDF) extend classical tree ensembles by replacing point-valued leaf predictions with \emph{probabilistic leaf distributions} and selecting partitions using \emph{log predictive scores}. We frame BDF as an \emph{evidence-centered forest architecture} rather than a strictly conjugate Bayesian method: when the leaf family is conjugate (e.g., Normal--Normal for real-valued targets, Beta--Bernoulli for binary targets, Gamma--Poisson for counts), the leaf posterior, posterior predictive, and marginal likelihood are available in closed form, all updates reduce to low-dimensional sufficient statistics, and the split score is the exact negative log evidence (NLE). When the leaf family is non-conjugate---in particular the Student-$t$ leaf that is selected on the majority of regression datasets in our experiments (Table~\ref{tab:bdf-selection})---closed-form evidence is unavailable; BDF then uses a BIC-style complexity correction to plug-in NLL (a Laplace/BIC evidence surrogate when the plug-in fit is an MLE/MAP, and a heuristic regularizer for the default method-of-moments Student-$t$ fit), or, as a pragmatic log-score variant, the plug-in negative log-likelihood whose missing complexity term is absorbed by the tempered MDL-style split penalty in Eq.~\eqref{eq:net_gain}, the minimum-leaf-size constraint, ensembling, and the distribution-selection step. KDE leaves analogously use LOO-corrected predictive scores. Predictions from non-conjugate leaves use the fitted leaf distribution rather than a posterior predictive.

We emphasize at the outset that the overall procedure---greedy search, empirical Bayes hyperparameters, tempered selection penalties---involves pragmatic approximations that depart from a fully Bayesian treatment of the tree structure. The leaf-level inference is exactly Bayesian only when the leaf family is conjugate; for non-conjugate leaves the Bayesian interpretation is asymptotic (Laplace/BIC) or evidence-regularized rather than exact, and we are explicit about which guarantees apply where. This design is deliberate: it captures the primary benefits of Bayesian leaf modeling (uncertainty quantification, automatic Occam penalties) where they are cheap, retains the scalability of frequentist tree algorithms, and exposes an architecture in which the regularizers responsible for split control are layered---prior-based shrinkage in conjugate leaves, the Laplace/BIC complexity term in non-conjugate leaves, and the global MDL-style penalty for all leaves---so that no single component is solely responsible for preventing overfitting.

\subsection{Problem Setup and Partitions}

Let $\mathcal{D}_n = \{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$, with $\mathbf{x}_i \in \mathcal{X}\subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ and $y_i \in \mathcal{Y}$.
A decision tree $T$ defines a partition of $\mathcal{X}$ into leaf regions $\{R_\ell\}_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}(T)}$, where $\bigcup_{\ell} R_\ell = \mathcal X$ and $R_i \cap R_j = \emptyset$ for all distinct $i, j \in \mathcal L(T)$, with $\mathcal{L}(T)$ denoting the set of leaves.
Each leaf $\ell$ corresponds to the local dataset
\[
\mathcal{D}_\ell = \{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\in \mathcal{D}_n:\ \mathbf{x}_i\in R_\ell\},
\qquad n_\ell = |\mathcal{D}_\ell|.
\]
For a query point $\mathbf{x}^\star$, let $\ell_T(\mathbf{x}^\star)$ denote its leaf assignment in $T$.

\subsection{Leaf Models and Bayesian Updates}\label{subsec:leaf_models}

Within each leaf $\ell$, BDF specifies a conditional model for $Y$ that is constant within the region:
\[
y \mid (\mathbf{x}\in R_\ell, \theta_\ell) \sim p(y \mid \theta_\ell),
\qquad
\theta_\ell \sim \pi(\theta_\ell \mid \eta),
\]
where $\theta_\ell$ are leaf parameters and $\eta$ are global hyperparameters shared across leaves.
The leaf posterior is
\[
\pi(\theta_\ell \mid \mathcal{D}_\ell, \eta)
\ \propto\
\pi(\theta_\ell \mid \eta)\prod_{(\mathbf{x}_i,y_i)\in\mathcal{D}_\ell} p(y_i\mid\theta_\ell).
\]
The leaf posterior predictive distribution is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:leaf_ppd}
p_\ell(y^\star \mid \mathcal{D}_\ell, \eta)
=
\int p(y^\star \mid \theta_\ell)\,\pi(\theta_\ell \mid \mathcal{D}_\ell,\eta)\,d\theta_\ell.
\end{equation}
Conditional on the leaf parameter $\theta_\ell$ and the leaf assignment, observations within each leaf are i.i.d.; this conditional independence---rather than a separate exchangeability assumption---is what licenses the prequential factorization of the marginal likelihood (Section~\ref{subsec:split_scoring}). We note that this i.i.d.\ property holds \emph{conditional on a fixed partition}; when the partition is learned from data, the observations reaching a given leaf are selected by a data-dependent rule, and the i.i.d.\ assumption is only approximate. We return to this point in Section~\ref{subsec:theory_adaptive_caveat}.

\paragraph{Conjugate leaves.}
For conjugate likelihood--prior pairs (e.g.\ Normal--Normal, Beta--Bernoulli), the posterior, posterior predictive, and marginal likelihood are available in closed form. All updates reduce to sufficient statistics, enabling constant-time leaf re-scoring during split search. Concrete update equations for the leaf families used in this paper are collected in Appendix~\ref{app:conjugate_updates}.

\paragraph{Non-conjugate leaves.}
When the likelihood--prior pair is not conjugate, closed-form expressions are generally unavailable. BDF then requires only that each candidate leaf admits a tractable predictive density and an additive split score that can be evaluated during split search. The architecture supports three score variants for such leaves, exposed as a tunable choice during hyperparameter search:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Laplace/BIC evidence surrogate}---plug-in negative log-likelihood plus $\tfrac{k_\ell}{2}\log n_\ell$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:bic_leaf}, justified as an $O(1)$ approximation to the NLE under standard regularity conditions when the plug-in estimate is an MLE/MAP (Section~\ref{subsec:theory_surrogates});
\item \emph{LOO- or $K$-fold-corrected predictive score}---used for KDE leaves where the BIC penalty is ill-defined ($k=1$ is not informative) but the leaf admits cheap leave-one-out updates;
\item \emph{Pure plug-in negative log-likelihood}---a pragmatic log-score variant whose missing complexity term is absorbed by the architecture's tempered MDL-style split penalty in Eq.~\eqref{eq:net_gain}, the minimum-leaf-size constraint, the depth-dependent prior on tree growth, ensembling, and the distribution-selection step.
\end{enumerate}
Predictions from a non-conjugate leaf use its fitted leaf distribution rather than a posterior predictive. The Bayesian interpretation of the split criterion is exact for conjugate leaves, asymptotic (Laplace/BIC) for variant (i), and evidence-regularized rather than fully Bayesian for variants (ii) and (iii); the experiments report which variant is selected per dataset (Table~\ref{tab:bdf-selection}). In the benchmark configs these choices are encoded by a single effective \texttt{score\_regime}: \texttt{nle} means exact negative log evidence with no BIC correction, \texttt{nll\_bic} means plug-in NLL plus the BIC-style correction, and \texttt{nll} means pure plug-in NLL. Concrete leaf specifications used in experiments are summarized in Appendix~\ref{app:conjugate_updates} (conjugate leaves) and Appendix~\ref{app:nonconjugate_updates} (non-conjugate leaves), with the Laplace/BIC backing for the non-conjugate split score derived in Appendix~\ref{app:approximate-bayesian-calculations-non-conjugate}.

\paragraph{Empirical Bayes hyperparameters.}\label{par:empirical_bayes}
By default, $\eta$ is set from training data only, jointly with the other BDF hyperparameters on the tuning fold; the full protocol (search space, splits, and the separation between EB estimation and held-out evaluation) is given in Appendix~\ref{app:benchmark-protocol}. For the Normal--Normal leaf model $y \mid \mu,\sigma_\ell^2 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma_\ell^2)$, $\mu \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \tau_0^2)$, we set $\mu_0 = \bar{y}_n$ and treat $\tau_0^2$ as a tunable hyperparameter controlling the degree of shrinkage; the observation variance used in the leaf update is the leaf-local plug-in estimate $\hat\sigma_\ell^2=s_\ell^2$ computed from the observations reaching that leaf, with a small numerical floor. Thus the Normal--Normal update is conjugate conditional on $\hat\sigma_\ell^2$, and its score is a profile/empirical-Bayes evidence rather than a model that integrates over $\sigma_\ell^2$. For the Beta--Bernoulli model, the prior $\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha_0, \beta_0)$ is calibrated so that $\alpha_0/(\alpha_0+\beta_0)$ matches the empirical class frequency, while the prior's variance $\frac{\alpha_0\beta_0}{(\alpha_0+\beta_0)^2(\alpha_0+\beta_0+1)}$ is a tunable hyperparameter. In the main benchmarks, prior strength is therefore selected as part of validation rather than fixed by a universal rule; the separate ablations isolate tree regularization, minimum leaf size, and the scoring criterion, but do not constitute a one-at-a-time EB prior-strength study.

Because $\hat\eta$ is estimated from the training fold prior to tree construction, the priors across leaves are coupled through this plug-in estimate. As a result, the exact factorization of the marginal likelihood across leaves (eq.~\eqref{eq:global_nle_sum}) holds only conditional on $\eta$. In the theoretical development (Section~\ref{sec:theory}), we treat $\eta$ as fixed.

Under standard regularity conditions, $\hat\eta$ is $\sqrt{n}$-consistent. The impact of replacing $\eta$ with $\hat\eta$ on leaf-level log scores depends on the sensitivity of the marginal likelihood to $\eta$ and on the leaf size $n_\ell$. In regular conjugate models, this induces a perturbation that is asymptotically negligible relative to the $O(n_\ell)$ leading term governing split gains. We therefore view empirical Bayes as a practical approximation whose asymptotic effect is dominated by the data fit term, but we do not rely on a uniform $O_p(n^{-1/2})$ bound across leaves.

\subsection{Split Scoring}\label{subsec:split_scoring}

BDF selects splits by minimizing an additive tree-level negative log score,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:additive_tree_score}
\mathrm{Loss}(T;\mathcal{D}_n)
=
\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}(T)} S(\mathcal{D}_\ell),
\end{equation}
where $S(\mathcal{D}_\ell)$ is a leaf-level negative log score measuring the quality of the probabilistic predictor in leaf~$\ell$. For conjugate leaves, $S$ is the negative log marginal likelihood (evidence). For non-conjugate plug-in leaves, $S$ is the negative log-likelihood with an evidence surrogate such as BIC.

\paragraph{Negative log evidence (NLE) for conjugate leaves.}
The primary split criterion uses the marginal likelihood,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nle_leaf}
S(\mathcal{D}_\ell)
=
-\log m(\mathcal{D}_\ell),
\qquad
m(\mathcal{D}_\ell)
=
\int \pi(\theta_\ell\mid\eta)\prod_{i:\mathbf{x}_i\in R_\ell}p(y_i\mid\theta_\ell)\,d\theta_\ell.
\end{equation}
Conditional on the leaf assignment and on $\eta$, the observations in leaf $\ell$ are i.i.d.\ given $\theta_\ell$, so the marginal likelihood admits a prequential (sequential predictive) factorization:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prequential}
m(\mathcal{D}_\ell)
= \prod_{i=1}^{n_\ell} p(y_{\sigma(i)} \mid y_{\sigma(1)},\dots,y_{\sigma(i-1)},\eta),
\end{equation}
for any permutation $\sigma$ of the leaf indices, where each factor is the one-step-ahead posterior predictive. The NLE thus equals the cumulative log-loss of a sequentially updated Bayesian forecaster---a connection to prequential model validation that we formalize in Section~\ref{subsec:theory_prequential}.

\paragraph{BIC surrogate for non-conjugate leaves.}
When the marginal likelihood is not available in closed form, we use the Laplace/BIC surrogate
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bic_leaf}
S_{\mathrm{BIC}}(\mathcal{D}_\ell)
=
-\log p(\mathcal{D}_\ell \mid \hat{\theta}_\ell^{\,\mathrm{MAP}})
+
\frac{k_\ell}{2}\log n_\ell,
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\theta}_\ell^{\,\mathrm{MAP}}$ is the posterior mode when a prior is specified, the MLE for purely plug-in leaves, and $k_\ell = \dim(\theta_\ell)$. The MAP and MLE coincide asymptotically under regularity conditions, and the $O(1)$ difference between them is absorbed into the remainder of the Laplace expansion (Section~\ref{subsec:theory_surrogates}). Under standard regularity conditions, $S_{\mathrm{BIC}}$ approximates the NLE up to $O(1)$ error as $n_\ell \to \infty$ \citep{schwarz1978estimating}. When a non-conjugate leaf uses faster method-of-moments parameters instead of an MLE/MAP, as in the default Student-$t$ benchmark configuration, the same term should be read as a BIC-sized complexity correction rather than as a formal Laplace evidence approximation. A theoretical comparison of the two criteria, including finite-sample behavior and implications for split selection under misspecification, is given in Section~\ref{subsec:theory_surrogates}.

\paragraph{Predictive-score variants and pure plug-in NLL.}
The additive framework also permits replacing $S$ by a negative held-out or approximate out-of-sample log predictive score, such as LOO or $K$-fold, or by the pure plug-in negative log-likelihood without any Laplace/BIC complexity term. These variants are useful when the marginal likelihood is unavailable, too sensitive to prior specification, or empirically dominated by the architecture's other regularizers. In the experiments, the split-score variant is tuned alongside the leaf family on fold-0 CRPS: classification selects exact NLE on 8 of 12 datasets (matching the conjugate Beta--Bernoulli leaf), while regression selects exact NLE on only 2 of 15 datasets, NLL+BIC on 3, and pure plug-in NLL on the remaining 10, with the dominant configuration being a non-conjugate Student-$t$ leaf scored by plug-in NLL (Section~\ref{subsec:exp_real}, Table~\ref{tab:bdf-selection}). The conjugate Normal--Normal variant, where exact NLE is available, is within a few percent CRPS of the actually-selected variant on most regression datasets (median $+1.82\%$, Table~\ref{tab:bdf-conjugate-vs-selected-gap}), and the larger gaps are concentrated on heavy-tailed targets where the substantive gain comes from the Student-$t$ leaf family rather than from the split-score choice.

\paragraph{Split gain.}
Given a candidate split $s$ at leaf $\ell$ producing children $(\ell_L,\ell_R)$, the \emph{split gain} under score $S$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:split_gain}
\Delta S(s;\ell)
=
S(\mathcal{D}_\ell) - S(\mathcal{D}_{\ell_L}) - S(\mathcal{D}_{\ell_R}).
\end{equation}
Since $S$ is a negative log score (lower is better), a positive gain $\Delta S > 0$ indicates that the two-leaf model provides a better probabilistic fit than the unsplit leaf. Under independent priors on $\theta_{\ell_L}$ and $\theta_{\ell_R}$ (as assumed throughout), $\Delta S(s;\ell)$ equals the log Bayes factor in favor of splitting when $S$ is the NLE; this connection is formalized in Section~\ref{subsec:theory_split_bf}.

\paragraph{Relation to distributional forests.}
Likelihood-based distributional forests \citep{schlosser2019distributional} select splits by improvements in plug-in (pseudo-)likelihood using MLE leaf estimates, and Distributional Random Forests \citep[DRF;][]{cevid2022distributional} use maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) as the split criterion. BDF differs in two respects: (i) conjugate leaves produce full posterior predictive distributions~\eqref{eq:leaf_ppd}, while broader leaves expose explicit fitted predictive distributions such as Student-$t$ or KDE; and (ii) split selection optimizes a log-score objective that integrates out leaf parameters via the evidence~\eqref{eq:nle_leaf} where available, or uses a stated evidence surrogate or complexity correction otherwise.

Relative to DRF specifically, BDF's leaf object is an explicit parametric (or nonparametric, in the KDE case) predictive distribution rather than an implicit weighted-neighborhood smoother. The leaf family determines which distributional features can be captured \emph{within} a single leaf: Student-$t$ leaves accommodate heavy tails, Poisson and Gamma--Poisson leaves count data, Beta--Bernoulli leaves binary outcomes, and KDE leaves multimodal or otherwise non-parametric conditional densities. The forest mixture adds further flexibility on top of this. Split selection in DRF optimizes a distribution-free MMD criterion that can capture shifts no fixed parametric family represents, and this flexibility is empirically important: DRF and QRF match or exceed BDF in several real and synthetic comparisons. The evidence or evidence-surrogate criterion used by BDF directly penalizes complexity and rewards calibrated probabilistic fit when the leaf family is well chosen, so the comparison should be read as a tradeoff between nonparametric neighborhood flexibility and explicit likelihood-based predictive distributions. Compared with BART and the scalable variant XBART~\citep{he2019xbart}, BDF gives up posterior uncertainty over partition structures in exchange for greedy growth, training-fold EB hyperparameters, and randomized ensembling: BART quantifies tree-structure uncertainty through MCMC over topologies, whereas BDF's randomization comes from bootstrap and feature subsampling.


\subsection{Complexity Control and Greedy Growth}\label{subsec:complexity_growth}

We control tree complexity through a penalized splitting criterion. At each candidate leaf $\ell$ at depth $d$, we compare a \emph{stop model} $M_{\mathrm{stop}}$ (retain the current leaf) against a \emph{split model} $M_{\mathrm{split}}$ (partition into two children via candidate split $s$). Under factorized priors, the log posterior odds decompose as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:posterior_odds}
\log \frac{P(M_{\mathrm{split}} \mid \mathcal{D})}{P(M_{\mathrm{stop}} \mid \mathcal{D})}
\;=\;
\underbrace{\Delta S(s;\ell)}_{\text{data support}}
\;+\;
\underbrace{\log \frac{P(M_{\mathrm{split}}, s)}{P(M_{\mathrm{stop}})}}_{\text{prior log-odds}}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Structural prior and tempered selection penalty.}
The prior probability of splitting at depth $d$ follows a logistic model, $p(\mathrm{split} \mid d) = \sigma(-\alpha - \delta d)$, with $\alpha, \delta > 0$.
Conditional on splitting, we place a prior over split parameters $(j,c)$ by first selecting a feature uniformly and then a cutpoint uniformly among that feature's candidates. That is, $P(j) = 1/K$ and $P(c \mid j) = 1/m_j$, yielding a prior contribution $-\log K - \log m_j$ to the log-odds.

To account for dependence among candidates (e.g., correlated features or adjacent cutpoints inducing similar partitions), we introduce a tempering parameter $\gamma \in [0,1]$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:prior_logodds_fixed}
\log \frac{P(M_{\mathrm{split}}, s)}{P(M_{\mathrm{stop}})}
\;=\;
-\alpha - \delta d - \gamma(\log K + \log m_j).
\end{equation}
When $\gamma = 1$, this recovers the split-identity component of the Bayesian model selection penalty under the specified prior, up to the child-stopping terms discussed in Appendix~\ref{app:split_bayes_factor}. When $\gamma < 1$, the penalty is reduced to reflect an effective number of distinguishable candidates smaller than the nominal count; $\gamma=0$ removes the split-identity multiplicity correction while retaining the structural depth penalty.

Substituting~\eqref{eq:split_gain} and~\eqref{eq:prior_logodds_fixed} into~\eqref{eq:posterior_odds} yields the \emph{net gain}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:net_gain}
\Delta_{\mathrm{net}}(s;\ell)
\;=\;
\Delta S(s;\ell) - \gamma\log(K \cdot m_j) - \alpha - \delta d.
\end{equation}
A split is accepted if and only if $\Delta_{\mathrm{net}}(s;\ell) > 0$, i.e., the data support exceeds the combined structural and selection-complexity penalties.
The depth penalty $\alpha + \delta d$ with $\delta > 0$ discourages excessively deep trees by requiring progressively larger gains to justify further splits. A heuristic scaling argument illustrates the typical growth rate. A leaf at depth $d$ contains at most $n$ observations, so its split gain satisfies $\Delta S \le n_\ell \cdot B$ for a constant $B$ depending on the leaf family. For the split to be accepted, we require
\[
n_\ell \cdot B > \alpha + \delta d + \gamma(\log K + \log m_j).
\]
Under approximately balanced splits, $n_\ell \approx n / 2^{d-1}$, which yields the implicit condition
\[
\frac{n B}{2^{d-1}} \gtrsim \delta d.
\]
This relation implies a typical depth scaling $d = O(\log n)$ under balanced partitioning. We emphasize that this is a heuristic scaling law rather than a formal upper bound: highly unbalanced splits can produce deeper paths, although such paths are discouraged by both diminishing sample sizes and the depth penalty.

\begin{remark}[Interpretation via description length]\label{rem:mdl}
The net gain~\eqref{eq:net_gain} admits a minimum description length (MDL) interpretation. The term $\Delta S$ is the reduction in data coding cost under the prequential code~\eqref{eq:prequential}. The remaining terms form a structural code: $\alpha + \delta d$ nats to signal a split at depth $d$, and $\gamma\log(K \cdot m_j)$ nats to encode the split identity. The linear depth penalty corresponds to a geometric prior on tree depth (the maximum-entropy distribution subject to a finite expected-depth constraint). The parameter $\gamma$ controls the assumed coding precision: $\gamma = 1$ assigns the full split-identity code length, $\gamma < 1$ assigns a shorter code reflecting candidate redundancy, and $\gamma=0$ omits this code. BART \citep{chipman2010bart} uses the branching-process prior $p(\text{split} \mid d) = \alpha'(1+d)^{-\beta'}$, yielding a heavier-tailed depth distribution; we find that the exponential tail of the logistic prior provides sufficient flexibility under greedy growth with early stopping (Appendix~\ref{app:additional-param-sensitivity}).
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Hyperparameter selection.}
The penalty parameters $(\alpha, \delta, \gamma)$ are selected on the tuning fold jointly with the remaining BDF hyperparameters; see Appendix~\ref{app:benchmark-protocol} for the search space, objective, and split protocol. The implementation defaults are $\alpha = 0$, $\delta = 0.001$, $\gamma = 0.1$ and are used only when no tuning fold is available. We find empirically that $\gamma < 1$ improves performance on datasets with correlated features or finely discretized cutpoints, consistent with the effective-multiplicity interpretation (Appendix~\ref{app:additional-param-sensitivity}).

\paragraph{Greedy construction.}
We implement the criterion~\eqref{eq:net_gain} within a best-first greedy strategy (Algorithm~\ref{alg:bdf_tree}), which maintains a priority queue of candidate splits ordered by $\Delta_{\mathrm{net}}$ and expands the highest-gain split at each step.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Greedy construction of a single Bayesian distributional tree}\label{alg:bdf_tree}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Dataset $\mathcal{D}$, hyperparameters $\eta$, penalty parameters $(\alpha, \delta, \gamma)$
\STATE Initialize tree $T$ as a single root leaf $\ell_0$; compute sufficient statistics, the leaf posterior or fitted parameters, and leaf score $S(\mathcal{D}_{\ell_0})$
\STATE Find the best candidate split $s^*$ at $\ell_0$ maximizing $\Delta_{\mathrm{net}}(s;\ell_0)$ via~\eqref{eq:net_gain}
\IF{$\Delta_{\mathrm{net}}(s^*;\ell_0) > 0$}
    \STATE Push $(\ell_0, s^*, \Delta_{\mathrm{net}})$ onto priority queue $\mathcal{Q}$
\ENDIF
\WHILE{$\mathcal{Q}$ is not empty}
    \STATE Pop $(\ell, s, \Delta)$ with largest $\Delta$ from $\mathcal{Q}$
    \STATE Apply split $s$ to $\ell$, creating children $\ell_L, \ell_R$
    \FOR{each child $c \in \{\ell_L, \ell_R\}$}
        \STATE Compute sufficient statistics, the leaf posterior or fitted parameters, and score $S(\mathcal{D}_c)$
        \STATE Find the best candidate split $s_c$ at depth $d_c$ maximizing $\Delta_{\mathrm{net}}(s_c; c)$
        \IF{$\Delta_{\mathrm{net}}(s_c; c) > 0$}
            \STATE Push $(c, s_c, \Delta_{\mathrm{net}})$ onto $\mathcal{Q}$
        \ENDIF
    \ENDFOR
\ENDWHILE
\RETURN Tree $T$ with leaf predictive objects $\{p_\ell(\cdot \mid \mathcal{D}_\ell,\eta)\}_{\ell \in \mathcal{L}(T)}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


\subsection{Ensembles and Probabilistic Prediction}\label{subsec:ensemble}

A Bayesian Distributional Forest is an ensemble of $M$ randomized trees $\{T^{(m)}\}_{m=1}^M$ trained independently (and thus fully parallelizable) using standard diversity mechanisms: bootstrap or row subsampling of training data, and random feature subsampling at each split.

For a test input $\mathbf{x}^\star$, each tree contributes the predictive distribution from its assigned leaf,
\[
p^{(m)}(y^\star\mid \mathbf{x}^\star,\mathcal{D}_n)
=
p_{\ell_{T^{(m)}}(\mathbf{x}^\star)}(y^\star \mid \mathcal{D}_{\ell_{T^{(m)}}(\mathbf{x}^\star)},\eta).
\]
BDF aggregates these as a uniform mixture:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:forest_mixture}
p_{\mathrm{BDF}}(y^\star\mid \mathbf{x}^\star,\mathcal{D}_n)
=
\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^M p^{(m)}(y^\star\mid \mathbf{x}^\star,\mathcal{D}_n).
\end{equation}
This mixture can represent complex predictive shapes (e.g., multimodality, skewness) even when individual leaves are simple parametric families.

Equation~\eqref{eq:forest_mixture} is a frequentist ensemble of probabilistic leaf predictors: conjugate leaves provide exact posterior predictives, while plug-in or KDE leaves provide the fitted predictive distribution specified by their leaf family. This is distinct from full Bayesian model averaging over tree topologies, as pursued in BART \citep{chipman2010bart}. BART places a prior over the space of tree structures and uses MCMC to approximate the posterior over topologies, yielding (in principle) coherent posterior uncertainty over the partition itself. BDF instead fixes each tree greedily and averages over the randomization in the bootstrap and feature subsampling, trading full posterior coherence over tree structures for computational scalability and parallelism. The Bayesian character of BDF resides in conjugate leaf-level inference and the evidence-based split criterion; for broader plug-in leaves it is an evidence-regularized greedy forest.

\section{Theoretical Perspective}\label{sec:theory}

This section records the main guarantees needed to interpret BDF. The results are deliberately local: they condition on a fixed tree partition and fixed hyperparameters $\eta$, and therefore justify the leaf predictors and split scores rather than proving consistency of the full adaptive forest. Detailed proofs and auxiliary asymptotics are in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.

\subsection{Evidence as a Prequential Log Score}\label{subsec:theory_prequential}

For a fixed tree $T$, the prior factorizes over leaves,
$\pi(\theta\mid \eta,T)=\prod_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}(T)}\pi(\theta_\ell\mid\eta)$, and the marginal likelihood factorizes as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:global_nle_sum}
-\log p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X},T,\eta)
=
\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}(T)} S(\mathcal{D}_\ell),
\qquad
S(\mathcal{D}_\ell)=-\log m(\mathcal{D}_\ell).
\end{equation}
Within each leaf, $m(\mathcal{D}_\ell)$ also has the prequential representation
\[
m(\mathcal{D}_\ell)
=
\prod_{j=1}^{n_\ell} p(y_{\ell,j}\mid y_{\ell,1:j-1}, \eta),
\]
for any ordering of the observations in the leaf. Thus the NLE used for split scoring is exactly the cumulative log-loss of a sequential Bayesian forecaster. Since log-loss is strictly proper,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:proper_log_score}
\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)\sim P_0}\big[-\log q(y\mid \mathbf{x})\big]
\ge
\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)\sim P_0}\big[-\log f_0(y\mid \mathbf{x})\big],
\end{equation}
with equality only at the true conditional density, minimizing expected NLE targets conditional KL risk for a fixed partition.

\subsection{Evidence, Complexity, and Surrogates}
\label{subsec:theory_occam}
\label{subsec:theory_surrogates}
\label{subsec:theory_tempered}
\label{subsec:theory_split_bf}

The evidence includes an automatic Occam penalty. For the exact leaf posterior,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:evidence_decomposition}
-\log m(\mathcal{D}_\ell)
=
\underbrace{-\mathbb{E}_{\pi(\theta_\ell\mid \mathcal{D}_\ell)}
\big[\log p(\mathcal{D}_\ell\mid \theta_\ell)\big]}_{\text{posterior expected negative log-lik.}}
\;+\;
\underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi(\theta_\ell\mid \mathcal{D}_\ell)\,\Vert\,\pi(\theta_\ell\mid\eta)\right)}_{\text{Occam factor}}.
\end{equation}
This identity is the Bayesian source of small-leaf regularization: a split must improve fit enough to justify moving two child posteriors away from the prior rather than one parent posterior. The explicit threshold in~\eqref{eq:net_gain} adds a separate structural and search penalty. When $\gamma=1$ it corresponds to the uniform prior over split identities; when $\gamma<1$ it is a tempered effective-multiplicity correction for dependent cutpoints and correlated features. The tempered criterion should therefore be read as a penalized Bayes-factor split rule, not as exact posterior odds under a normalized prior over all candidate trees. We treat $\gamma$ as a predictive regularization parameter and tune it by validation log predictive density.

For non-conjugate leaves fitted by MLE/MAP, BDF replaces the evidence by the BIC/Laplace surrogate from~\eqref{eq:bic_leaf}. Under standard regularity conditions,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:laplace_bic_theory}
-\log m(\mathcal{D}_\ell)
=
-\log p(\mathcal{D}_\ell\mid \hat{\theta}_\ell)
+
\frac{k_\ell}{2}\log n_\ell
+
O(1),
\end{equation}
so the approximation error in a split gain is lower order than the $O(n_\ell)$ signal of a beneficial split. For method-of-moments non-conjugate leaves such as the default Student-$t$ benchmark configuration, the same $\tfrac{k_\ell}{2}\log n_\ell$ term is used only as a BIC-sized complexity correction. When BDF further drops this term and scores splits with the pure plug-in NLL---a configuration that fold-0 tuning selects on 10 of 15 regression datasets in our experiments---the Bayesian guarantee in Eq.~\eqref{eq:laplace_bic_theory} is no longer in force, and the complexity-control role passes to the architecture's tempered MDL-style structural penalty in Eq.~\eqref{eq:net_gain}, the minimum-leaf-size constraint, the depth-dependent prior on tree growth, and ensembling. The empirical comparison between NLE, NLL+BIC, and pure NLL on the conjugate Normal--Normal leaf shows the three scores agree to within $\sim 10^{-3}$ in skill score on every dataset tested (Appendix~\ref{app:scoring-method-full}, Table~\ref{tab:a2_real_data}), and the conjugate Normal--Normal variant trails the actually-selected variant by a median of $+1.82\%$ in CRPS (Table~\ref{tab:bdf-conjugate-vs-selected-gap}), so the pragmatic plug-in NLL regime is well behaved in practice even though it is not Bayes-exact. Under independent child priors, the NLE gain is also the log Bayes factor for splitting:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bf_local}
\log \mathrm{BF}(s)
=
\log m(\mathcal{D}_{\Omega_L})+\log m(\mathcal{D}_{\Omega_R})-\log m(\mathcal{D}_\Omega)
=
\Delta S(s;\Omega),
\end{equation}
and BDF accepts a split precisely when
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bf_threshold}
\log \mathrm{BF}(s) >
\gamma\log(K \cdot m_j)+\alpha+\delta d .
\end{equation}
Appendix~\ref{app:bic_uniform} and Appendix~\ref{app:split_consistency} give the full Laplace and per-node split-selection arguments. These arguments are local to a candidate node and do not turn the greedy forest-construction procedure into posterior inference over tree structures.

\subsection{Leaf Predictive Consistency}\label{subsec:theory_leaf_consistency}

For a fixed region $\Omega$, leaf inference with an explicit Bayesian posterior concentrates around the best-in-class density as the number of observations in that region grows.

\begin{proposition}[Leaf-level posterior concentration]\label{prop:leaf_concentration}
Let $\Omega \subset \mathcal{X}$ be fixed, and suppose that conditional on $\mathbf{X}\in\Omega$, responses are i.i.d.\ from density $f_0^\Omega$. Let $\{p_\theta:\theta\in\Theta\}$ be a regular parametric leaf family with compact parameter space, smooth identifiable likelihood, positive definite local information at the well-specified or KL-optimal parameter $\theta^\star$, and prior density positive and continuous near $\theta^\star$. Then, as $n_\Omega\to\infty$, the posterior concentrates around $\theta^\star$. In the well-specified case it satisfies the usual Bernstein--von Mises approximation; under misspecification it concentrates around the KL projection. Consequently,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ppd_convergence}
\int |p_\ell(y \mid \mathcal{D}_\Omega, \eta) - p_{\theta^\star}(y)|\,dy \to 0 .
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

The proposition says that, conditional on the partition, each conjugate or explicitly Bayesian leaf predictive is asymptotically optimal within its chosen family. For non-conjugate plug-in leaves, the analogous target is the fitted family density selected by the regularized log-score procedure rather than an exact posterior predictive. Under misspecification, these are KL-projection statements rather than calibration guarantees; empirical coverage is therefore assessed directly in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}. The full proof and verification for the Bayesian leaf families used here are in Appendix~\ref{app:proof_leaf_concentration}.

\subsection{Adaptive Partitions and Forest Mixtures}
\label{subsec:theory_adaptive_caveat}
\label{subsec:theory_forest}

The fixed-partition scope is important. In the actual algorithm, the data choose the partition and also fit either leaf posteriors or plug-in leaf parameters, so the theory above does not establish post-selection consistency of the greedily grown forest. Proving such a result would require controlling the complexity of the partitions reachable by best-first tree growth. BDF instead uses evidence penalties, validation-tuned structural penalties, row/feature subsampling, and ensembling to control this adaptivity in practice.

The forest mixture still has a simple log-loss property. For any test pair $(\mathbf{x},y)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mixture_jensen}
-\log p_{\mathrm{BDF}}(y \mid \mathbf{x})
= -\log\!\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_m p^{(m)}(y \mid \mathbf{x})\right)
\le \frac{1}{M}\sum_m \left[-\log p^{(m)}(y \mid \mathbf{x})\right],
\end{equation}
by Jensen's inequality. Thus mixture aggregation cannot be worse, in pointwise log-loss, than the average tree predictive. This does not make BDF a fully Bayesian posterior over tree structures; it remains a randomized ensemble of probabilistic leaf predictors, exact-Bayesian only for the conjugate leaf components.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}\label{sec:discussion}

We introduced Bayesian Distributional Forests (BDF), an evidence-centered probabilistic tree ensemble that performs exact Bayesian inference within conjugate leaves, uses Laplace/BIC evidence surrogates for regular MLE/MAP non-conjugate leaves, uses BIC-style complexity corrections for faster plug-in leaves such as the default MoM Student-$t$, and---when the leaf family is well chosen and the architecture's other regularizers absorb the missing complexity term---admits pure plug-in NLL as a pragmatic log-score variant. The resulting predictor is not a full Bayesian posterior over tree structures; it is a randomized forest whose Bayesian component resides in the leaf posteriors and posterior predictives where they apply, and in evidence-regularized split scoring elsewhere. This design keeps the training loop close to scalable tree induction while producing full predictive distributions.

The empirical results place BDF in a favorable accuracy--calibration tradeoff: it is the strongest parametric probabilistic regression model by relative CRPS and has the best 90\% interval-score rank, while QRF remains the aggregate CRPS/CRPSS leader and DRF is also slightly ahead of BDF. The synthetic DGP study reinforces the same reading rather than overturning it: DRF, QRF, BDF-KDE, and some parametric boosters are all close, with DRF best on the bimodal mixture and QRF marginally best on the step-function DGP. This ordering is unsurprising: QRF and DRF use flexible forest neighborhoods with empirical or weighted conditional distributions, whereas BDF trades some nonparametric flexibility for explicit leaf likelihoods, Bayesian leaf uncertainty where available, and log-score-based split selection. The broader story is therefore modularity and statistical coherence rather than universal dominance: BDF gives practitioners a single tree-ensemble template in which the predictive distribution is an explicit modeling choice across regression, classification, and richer leaf families. That matters in applications where the density itself, tail behavior, count structure, or domain-constrained likelihood families are part of the modeling target rather than just a route to lower CRPS.

Several limitations remain. BDF depends on the chosen leaf family and prior strength, so misspecification can affect both sharpness and calibration in low-data regions; tuning selects a non-conjugate Student-$t$ leaf family on the majority of regression datasets (12 of 15), NLL+BIC on 3 of those 12 Student-$t$ selections, and pure plug-in NLL on the remaining 9 Student-$t$ selections. Thus the architecture's practical regression regime is often evidence-regularized rather than strictly conjugate-Bayes. The theory in this paper justifies the leaf predictors and split scores under fixed partitions, but does not prove consistency of the full adaptive forest. Non-conjugate MLE/MAP leaves rely on Laplace/BIC approximations whose finite-sample split-selection behavior is only partly characterized, while MoM Student-$t$ leaves use the BIC term as a heuristic complexity correction; when pure plug-in NLL is used, the complexity-control role shifts to the tempered MDL-style split penalty, the minimum-leaf-size constraint, and ensembling, all of which depend on tuning rather than on a closed-form Bayesian guarantee. On the strongest nonparametric forest baselines, QRF leads aggregate CRPS/CRPSS and DRF is slightly ahead of BDF; BDF remains the strongest parametric distributional baseline and has the best 90\% interval-score rank, but is not the universal SOTA across all distributional metrics.

A further practical limitation is prediction-time cost: BDF's per-query overhead relative to standard random forests stems from three architectural choices. First, BDF ensembles are typically larger than RF, because each tree contributes a full leaf distribution rather than a point estimate, and stabilizing the predictive \emph{distribution} requires more trees than stabilizing the mean. Second, evaluation of the forest mixture~\eqref{eq:forest_mixture} performs per-tree, per-distribution work---sampling from each leaf's predictive distribution or computing analytic moments through the distribution interface---rather than reading a precomputed scalar leaf summary. Third, BDF leaves expose parameter sets rather than predict-time caches: even when the predictive mean is among the stored parameters (as for Normal leaves), point predictions are routed through the distribution object and computed from its parameterization, so there is no zero-cost mean lookup analogous to RF. Practical accelerations---caching leaf moments, distilling the mixture into a smaller parametric form, or evaluating fewer trees at inference---are natural and orthogonal to the training-time contributions of this paper, and we leave them to future work. These limitations point to natural extensions: richer leaf families, hierarchical or adaptive priors, stronger post-selection theory for greedy partitions, and decision-theoretic uses of the resulting predictive distributions.

Because BDF is a general-purpose uncertainty estimator for tabular prediction, its main societal risks arise through downstream use rather than through the algorithm in isolation. Predictive uncertainty can support risk-aware decisions, but it does not remove dataset bias, privacy concerns, or the need for domain validation. High-stakes deployments should therefore pair BDF with task-specific evaluation, calibration checks on the deployment population, and appropriate human oversight.

\section{Conjugate Leaf Models}\label{app:conjugate_updates}

For each conjugate family used in experiments we record the posterior,
posterior predictive, log marginal likelihood, and sufficient statistics.
All updates are $O(1)$ per observation.

\subsection{Normal--Normal Mean Model with Leaf-Local Plug-in Variance}\label{app:conjugate_normal}

Let $\bar{y}_\ell = n_\ell^{-1}\sum_{i:\mathbf{x}_i\in R_\ell} y_i$ denote the leaf sample mean and let $\hat\sigma_\ell^2=s_\ell^2$ denote the Bessel-corrected sample variance in the leaf, floored at a small positive constant for numerical stability. Conditional on this plug-in variance, the mean update is the usual conjugate Normal--Normal update. The sufficient statistics are $(n_\ell, \sum_i y_i, \sum_i y_i^2)$ and are maintained in $O(1)$ per insertion or removal, so both $\bar y_\ell$ and $\hat\sigma_\ell^2$ are available during split search.

\paragraph{Posterior.}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normal_posterior}
\tau_\ell^{-2} \;=\; \tau_0^{-2} + \frac{n_\ell}{\hat\sigma_\ell^2},
\qquad
\hat{\mu}_\ell \;=\; \tau_\ell^{2}\!\left(\frac{\mu_0}{\tau_0^2} + \frac{n_\ell\,\bar{y}_\ell}{\hat\sigma_\ell^2}\right),
\qquad
\mu \mid \mathcal{D}_\ell,\eta \sim \mathcal{N}(\hat{\mu}_\ell,\tau_\ell^2).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Posterior predictive.}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normal_predictive}
p_\ell(y^\star \mid \mathcal{D}_\ell,\eta) \;=\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(y^\star;\; \hat{\mu}_\ell,\; \hat\sigma_\ell^2 + \tau_\ell^2\right).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Profile log marginal likelihood.}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normal_log_marginal}
\begin{aligned}
\log m(\mathcal{D}_\ell)
\;=\;& -\frac{1}{2}\log\!\left(2\pi\left(\frac{\hat\sigma_\ell^2}{n_\ell}+\tau_0^2\right)\right)
-\frac{(\bar y_\ell-\mu_0)^2}{2(\hat\sigma_\ell^2/n_\ell+\tau_0^2)} \\
&\;-\frac{n_\ell-1}{2}\left(1+\log(2\pi\hat\sigma_\ell^2)\right).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
This is the evidence of the Normal mean model conditional on the leaf-local plug-in variance estimate, with the residual term evaluated at $\hat\sigma_\ell^2$; it is therefore an empirical-Bayes/profile evidence, not an integration over unknown variance.

\paragraph{Domination envelope.}
Let $\Theta = [\mu_{\min}, \mu_{\max}]$ be the compact mean interval required by Proposition~\ref{prop:leaf_concentration}(i), and condition on any leaf variance estimate bounded below by the numerical floor. Then $\sup_{\mu\in\Theta} \mathcal{N}(y;\mu,\hat\sigma_\ell^2) \le (2\pi\hat\sigma_\ell^2)^{-1/2}$ is finite; the corresponding fixed-variance envelope is invoked in Appendix~\ref{app:proof_leaf_concentration}(d) for the predictive $L^1$ convergence argument.

\begin{remark}[Plug-in observation variance]
The implementation estimates $\hat\sigma_\ell^2$ separately inside each candidate leaf from the data routed to that leaf. Consequently, the Normal--Normal benchmark should be read as exact Bayesian inference for the mean conditional on a leaf-local empirical-Bayes variance estimate; the Normal--Inverse-Gamma leaf is the fully conjugate alternative when variance uncertainty is integrated explicitly.
\end{remark}


\subsection{Beta--Bernoulli}\label{app:conjugate_beta}

Let $s_\ell = \sum_{i:\mathbf{x}_i\in R_\ell} y_i$ denote the count of positive outcomes; the sufficient statistics are $(n_\ell, s_\ell)$ and are maintained in $O(1)$.

\paragraph{Posterior.}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:beta_posterior}
p \mid \mathcal{D}_\ell,\eta \;\sim\; \mathrm{Beta}\!\left(\alpha_0 + s_\ell,\; \beta_0 + n_\ell - s_\ell\right).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Posterior predictive.}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:beta_predictive}
p_\ell(y^\star = 1 \mid \mathcal{D}_\ell,\eta) \;=\; \frac{\alpha_0 + s_\ell}{\alpha_0 + \beta_0 + n_\ell}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Log marginal likelihood.}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:beta_log_marginal}
\log m(\mathcal{D}_\ell)
\;=\; \log B\!\left(\alpha_0 + s_\ell,\; \beta_0 + n_\ell - s_\ell\right) - \log B(\alpha_0, \beta_0),
\end{equation}
where $B(a,b) = \Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)/\Gamma(a+b)$ is the Beta function.

\paragraph{Domination envelope.} Since $\mathcal{Y}=\{0,1\}$, $\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}p(y\mid\theta)\le 1$ and $g\equiv 1$ is trivially integrable on the two-point sample space.

\subsection{Gamma--Poisson Count Leaves}\label{app:conjugate_poisson}

For non-negative integer targets, BDF uses the conjugate Gamma--Poisson model. We write the Gamma prior in shape-rate form,
\[
\lambda \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha_0,\beta_0),
\qquad
y_i\mid \lambda \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda),
\]
where $\mathbb{E}[\lambda]=\alpha_0/\beta_0$ and $\mathrm{Var}(\lambda)=\alpha_0/\beta_0^2$. The implementation also exposes the equivalent mean-variance parameterization
\[
\alpha_0=\mu_\lambda^2/v_\lambda,
\qquad
\beta_0=\mu_\lambda/v_\lambda,
\]
with \texttt{auto} values set from training-fold moments and tuned prior scale.

\paragraph{Posterior.}
Let $s_\ell=\sum_{i:\mathbf{x}_i\in R_\ell} y_i$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:poisson_posterior}
\lambda\mid\mathcal{D}_\ell,\eta
\sim
\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha_\ell,\beta_\ell),
\qquad
\alpha_\ell=\alpha_0+s_\ell,
\quad
\beta_\ell=\beta_0+n_\ell .
\end{equation}
The sufficient statistics are $(n_\ell,s_\ell)$.

\paragraph{Posterior predictive.}
Integrating out $\lambda$ gives a Negative-Binomial predictive distribution,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:poisson_predictive}
p_\ell(y^\star=k\mid\mathcal{D}_\ell,\eta)
=
\mathrm{NegBin}\!\left(k;\alpha_\ell,\frac{\beta_\ell}{1+\beta_\ell}\right),
\end{equation}
where the second argument is the success probability in the SciPy convention. The predictive mean and variance are
\[
\mathbb{E}[Y^\star\mid\mathcal{D}_\ell]=\frac{\alpha_\ell}{\beta_\ell},
\qquad
\mathrm{Var}(Y^\star\mid\mathcal{D}_\ell)=
\frac{\alpha_\ell(1+\beta_\ell)}{\beta_\ell^2}.
\]

\paragraph{Log marginal likelihood.}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:poisson_log_marginal}
\log m(\mathcal{D}_\ell)
=
\log\Gamma(\alpha_\ell)-\log\Gamma(\alpha_0)
+\alpha_0\log\beta_0
-\alpha_\ell\log\beta_\ell
-\sum_i \log(y_i!).
\end{equation}

\section{Non-Conjugate Leaf Models}\label{app:nonconjugate_updates}

Tuning selects a non-conjugate leaf family on 12 of 15 regression datasets, dominated by the Student-$t$ leaf (Table~\ref{tab:bdf-selection}). These leaves do not admit a closed-form Bayesian evidence; BDF supports BIC-style complexity correction for plug-in likelihood scores, a leave-one-out predictive score for KDE, and a pure plug-in NLL variant whose missing complexity term is absorbed by the architecture's tempered MDL-style structural penalty (Eq.~\eqref{eq:net_gain}), the minimum-leaf-size constraint, and ensembling. The BIC correction is a Laplace evidence surrogate for regular MLE/MAP non-conjugate fits; for the default method-of-moments Student-$t$ benchmark configuration, it is a BIC-sized complexity penalty rather than a formal marginal-likelihood approximation. The score regime is exposed as a tunable categorical \{\texttt{nll\_bic}, \texttt{nll}\} for Student-$t$ leaves and analogous LOO/pure-NLL choices for KDE leaves, and is selected per dataset by fold-0 CRPS along with the other hyperparameters; for Student-$t$ leaves the locked artifacts select pure NLL on 9 of 12 datasets and NLL+BIC on 3 of 12. Predictions from non-conjugate leaves use the fitted leaf distribution rather than a posterior predictive.

\subsection{Student-\texorpdfstring{$t$}{t} Leaves}\label{app:student_t_leaves}

Student-$t$ leaves are used for robust real-valued regression and are the most frequently selected leaf family in the regression benchmarks.

\paragraph{Frequentist Student-$t$.}
The plug-in model is
\[
y_i \mid \mu,\sigma,\nu \sim t_\nu(\mu,\sigma),
\qquad
\theta=(\mu,\sigma,\nu),
\]
with $\nu>2$ when the variance is used. The implementation estimates $\mu$ and $\sigma$ by method of moments, EM/IRLS, or numerical MLE, and estimates $\nu$ when it is not fixed. The default benchmark configuration uses method-of-moments estimators
\begin{equation}\label{eq:student_t_mom}
\hat\mu_\ell = \bar y_\ell,
\qquad
\hat\sigma_\ell^{\,2}
= s_\ell^2 \cdot \frac{\hat\nu_\ell - 2}{\hat\nu_\ell},
\qquad
\hat\nu_\ell =
\begin{cases}
\nu_{\max}, & \hat\kappa_\ell \le 3,\\[2pt]
\mathrm{clip}\!\left(4 + \dfrac{6}{\hat\kappa_\ell - 3},\,\nu_{\min},\,\nu_{\max}\right), & \hat\kappa_\ell > 3,
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\bar y_\ell$ and $s_\ell^2$ are the leaf sample mean and (Bessel-corrected) sample variance, $\hat\kappa_\ell$ is the leaf sample (excess+3) kurtosis, and the implementation uses $(\nu_{\min},\nu_{\max})=(2.05,100)$. The positive-excess MoM formula is derived from the Student-$t$ excess-kurtosis identity $6/(\nu-4)$, so as empirical kurtosis grows the estimated $\nu$ approaches 4 from above. When $\hat\kappa_\ell \le 3$ (the leaf data are platykurtic), $\hat\nu_\ell$ saturates at the configured upper bound, and the leaf reduces to a near-Gaussian fit.

The split score is the plug-in negative log-likelihood with an optional BIC-style correction:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:student_t_score}
S_{\mathrm{t}}(\mathcal{D}_\ell;c)
=
-\sum_{i:\mathbf{x}_i\in R_\ell}
\log t_{\hat\nu_\ell}(y_i;\hat\mu_\ell,\hat\sigma_\ell)
\;+\;
c\cdot\frac{k_\ell}{2}\log n_\ell,
\qquad
c\in\{0,1\},
\end{equation}
where $c=1$ applies the BIC-style correction of Eq.~\eqref{eq:bic_leaf} and $c=0$ recovers the pure plug-in NLL discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:split_scoring}. The parameter dimension is $k_\ell=3$ when $\nu$ is estimated alongside $(\mu,\sigma)$ and $k_\ell=2$ when $\nu$ is fixed (e.g.\ to a configured default). For the method-of-moments estimator used in the locked Student-$t$ benchmark, this parameter count defines the size of the complexity correction; it should not be read as a full Laplace calculation around an MLE/MAP mode. The leaf predictive is the fitted density
\[
p_\ell(y^\star\mid\mathcal{D}_\ell)
=
t_{\hat\nu_\ell}(y^\star;\hat\mu_\ell,\hat\sigma_\ell),
\]
which is not a posterior predictive in the conjugate sense. With MLE/MAP parameters, the $c=1$ score has the Laplace interpretation of Appendix~\ref{app:approximate-bayesian-calculations-non-conjugate}; with the benchmark MoM parameters it is a BIC-sized regularization term. The $c=0$ score is the evidence-regularized log-score variant of Section~\ref{subsec:split_scoring}. In the locked artifacts, $c=0$ is selected on 9 of the 12 datasets where the Student-$t$ leaf is the chosen family.

\paragraph{Normal-mean Student-$t$.}
The \texttt{NormalMeanStudentT} variant adds shrinkage on the location parameter,
\[
\mu \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0,\sigma_0^2),
\qquad
y_i\mid\mu,\sigma,\nu\sim t_\nu(\mu,\sigma).
\]
Because this is still non-conjugate (the $t_\nu$ likelihood is not closed under a Gaussian prior on $\mu$), the implementation uses a normal-approximate update for the location and plug-in estimates for $(\sigma,\nu)$. Writing $s_\ell^2$ for the leaf sample variance and $\bar y_\ell$ for the leaf mean,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nmst_update}
v_{\mu,\ell}^{-1}=\sigma_0^{-2}+n_\ell/s_\ell^2,
\qquad
\hat\mu_\ell=v_{\mu,\ell}\left(\mu_0/\sigma_0^2+n_\ell\bar y_\ell/s_\ell^2\right).
\end{equation}
The update treats the within-leaf likelihood for $\mu$ as approximately Gaussian with precision $n_\ell/s_\ell^2$, which is the leading-order Laplace/CLT term in $n_\ell$ around the MLE $\bar y_\ell$ for a $t_\nu$ location model; the next-order correction is $O(n_\ell^{-1})$ in posterior mean for $\nu>2$ and is dropped by the implementation. Degrees of freedom are fixed or estimated from excess kurtosis as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:student_t_mom}, and $\sigma$ is recovered from
$\mathrm{Var}(Y)=\sigma^2\nu/(\nu-2)$. Splits again use Eq.~\eqref{eq:student_t_score}, now centered at the shrinkage estimate $\hat\mu_\ell$ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:nmst_update} rather than at $\bar y_\ell$.

\subsection{KDE Leaves}\label{app:kde_leaves}

KDE leaves are non-parametric predictive leaves used when a unimodal parametric likelihood is too restrictive. They do not have a closed-form Bayesian evidence in the current implementation.
For a leaf with centers $\{y_i\}_{i=1}^{n_\ell}$ and bandwidth $h_\ell>0$, the predictive density is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kde_predictive}
p_\ell(y^\star\mid\mathcal{D}_\ell,h_\ell)
=
\frac{1}{n_\ell h_\ell}\sum_{i=1}^{n_\ell}
K\!\left(\frac{y^\star-y_i}{h_\ell}\right),
\end{equation}
with Gaussian or Epanechnikov kernels. Bandwidth is either fixed, Scott/Silverman rule-of-thumb, or pseudo-Bayesian shrinkage toward a global bandwidth $h_0$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kde_bandwidth_shrinkage}
\log h_\ell^{\mathrm{post}}
=
\frac{m_h\log h_0+n_\ell\log h_\ell^{\mathrm{local}}}{m_h+n_\ell}.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Split score.}
The default KDE score is leave-one-out negative log predictive density,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kde_loo}
S_{\mathrm{KDE\text{-}LOO}}(\mathcal{D}_\ell)
=
-\sum_{i=1}^{n_\ell}
\log\left[
\frac{1}{(n_\ell-1)h_\ell}
\sum_{j\ne i}
K\!\left(\frac{y_i-y_j}{h_\ell}\right)
\right],
\end{equation}
which is the LOO/$K$-fold-corrected log-score variant of Section~\ref{subsec:split_scoring} specialized to a one-parameter kernel density estimator. The BIC penalty $\tfrac{k_\ell}{2}\log n_\ell$ with $k_\ell=1$ is not informative for KDE (one effective parameter, the bandwidth, that is selected from a discrete rule-of-thumb set), so it is excluded as an alternative; pure plug-in NLL is available as a fallback and is rarely selected in our experiments. For computational efficiency, the split search may use a parent bandwidth for all candidate children and refine the top candidate splits with child-specific bandwidths.

\paragraph{Mixture evaluation.}
For all sample-based leaves, including KDE and Student-$t$, the forest predictive in Eq.~\eqref{eq:forest_mixture} is evaluated by drawing from the assigned leaf distribution in each tree and pooling samples across trees. CRPS, PIT, central intervals, and interval scores are then computed from the pooled samples; quantile-native baselines such as QRF use their reported quantile grid directly.

\section{Laplace Approximation for Non-Conjugate Leaves}
\label{app:approximate-bayesian-calculations-non-conjugate}

When the likelihood--prior pair is not conjugate, a Laplace expansion around the MAP estimate gives the evidence surrogate used by the BIC split score. This section records that calculation; the locked Student-$t$ benchmark variants use method-of-moments fitted predictive densities at prediction time, as described in Appendix~\ref{app:student_t_leaves}, so their BIC term should be read as a BIC-sized complexity correction.

\paragraph{MAP estimate and curvature.}
Let $\ell_n(\theta_\ell) = \sum_{i:\mathbf{x}_i\in R_\ell} \log p(y_i\mid\theta_\ell)$ denote the leaf log-likelihood. The leaf posterior mode is
\[
\hat{\theta}_\ell^{\mathrm{MAP}} \;=\; \arg\max_{\theta\in\Theta}\, \big\{\log \pi(\theta\mid\eta) + \ell_n(\theta)\big\},
\]
obtained by a few iterations of Newton's method warm-started at the parent's MAP. The posterior curvature at the mode is
\[
H_\ell \;=\; -\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\!\left[\log\pi(\theta\mid\eta) + \ell_n(\theta)\right]\!\big|_{\theta=\hat{\theta}_\ell^{\mathrm{MAP}}}.
\]

\paragraph{Gaussian posterior approximation.}
Under the regularity conditions of Proposition~\ref{prop:leaf_concentration}, the Laplace approximation gives
\[
\pi(\theta_\ell\mid\mathcal{D}_\ell,\eta) \;\approx\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(\theta_\ell;\; \hat{\theta}_\ell^{\mathrm{MAP}},\; H_\ell^{-1}\right),
\]
with approximation error $o(1)$ in total variation as $n_\ell\to\infty$.

\paragraph{Approximate posterior predictive interpretation.}
If one carries the Laplace approximation through to prediction, the induced approximate posterior predictive would be
\[
p_\ell(y^\star\mid \mathcal{D}_\ell,\eta) \;\approx\; \int p(y^\star\mid\theta)\,\mathcal{N}\!\left(\theta;\,\hat{\theta}_\ell^{\mathrm{MAP}},\, H_\ell^{-1}\right) d\theta,
\]
evaluated in closed form for Gaussian-linearized likelihoods and numerically (Gauss--Hermite quadrature in low dimensions) otherwise. The locked Student-$t$ benchmark variants do not use this posterior-predictive integral at prediction time; they use the fitted Student-$t$ density described in Appendix~\ref{app:student_t_leaves}. In this paper the Laplace calculation justifies BIC evidence surrogates for MLE/MAP split scoring and motivates the BIC-sized correction used by faster plug-in variants, but it does not imply exact Bayesian prediction for the selected non-conjugate variants.

The associated BIC approximation to the log marginal likelihood, together with its validity as a surrogate for the NLE in split selection, is established in Appendix~\ref{app:bic_uniform}.
